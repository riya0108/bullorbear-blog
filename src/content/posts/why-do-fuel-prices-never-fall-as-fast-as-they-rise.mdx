**Why do Fuel prices never fall as fast as they rise?**
Economists have a name for what happens every time global oil prices fall but your petrol price barely moves. It is called the "rockets and feathers" effect — and once you understand it, you will never look at a fuel price revision the same way again.

<div class="not-prose my-8 grid grid-cols-1 gap-4 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div class="rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">
      <span aria-hidden="true">🚀</span> When Crude Rises
    </p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm font-bold text-ink">Prices at the pump go up fast</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-mute">Retail fuel prices increase sharply and almost immediately when wholesale oil costs rise — often within hours or days of a cost shock.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">
      <span aria-hidden="true">🪶</span> When Crude Falls
    </p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm font-bold text-ink">Prices at the pump drift down slowly</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-mute">When crude oil prices fall, the same retail prices take weeks or months to come down — and often never fully return to where they were before the rise.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## The Moment You Probably Already Know

It has happened to all of us. The news breaks: crude oil prices have surged due to a crisis in West Asia, or a hurricane in the Gulf of Mexico, or sanctions on a major producer. Within 48 hours — sometimes within 24 — petrol prices at the pump have jumped. The hike is real, it is immediate, and it is explained to you in press releases with charts and international price comparisons and technical language about "import parity."

Then, a few weeks or months later, the same news cycle brings a different headline: crude oil prices have fallen sharply. The crisis has eased. Supply is recovering. Global prices are down 15%, 20%, sometimes more. You wait for your petrol bill to reflect this good news. You wait a little longer. You check the pump price. It has come down, maybe — by ₹1.50, or ₹2, when it went up by ₹8. Or perhaps it has not come down at all.

You shrug. Life moves on. Something else captures your attention. And the pump price stays exactly where it was.

This is not an accident. It is not bad luck. It is not even, strictly speaking, illegal. It is a well-documented economic phenomenon that has been studied across dozens of countries, in dozens of commodity markets, for over fifty years. Economists call it the **"rockets and feathers" effect** — and understanding it is one of the most practically useful things any consumer can know about the economy they live in.

<div class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6 sm:p-7">
  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-mute">
    <span aria-hidden="true">📖</span> What the Research Shows
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">The rockets and feathers effect first gained widespread attention during the 1973 OPEC oil embargo, when crude oil prices quadrupled while retail price adjustments showed significant asymmetric lag patterns. Academic research by economist Severin Borenstein at UC Berkeley established the foundational framework for understanding these dynamics in the 1990s — demonstrating that retail petrol prices exhibit markedly different responses to cost increases versus decreases, and that the asymmetry extends far beyond simple cost-plus markup models.</p>
</div>

## The Mechanics: Why It Happens at All

Before examining why prices stay high after the trigger has faded, it is worth understanding the full supply chain that sits between a barrel of crude oil in the Persian Gulf and a litre of petrol in your tank. The pump price is not simply crude oil plus a markup. It is the end result of a chain with at least five distinct cost layers: crude oil itself, refining and crack spreads, distribution and storage, retail station operating costs, and finally taxes — both central excise and state VAT.

Each of these layers responds to cost changes at different speeds, in different directions, and with different degrees of transparency. The crude oil price is publicly available in real time on Bloomberg and Reuters. The refining margin — called the "crack spread" — is less visible but still tracked. The distribution cost and retail operating cost are almost entirely opaque to ordinary consumers. The tax component, as we have written about previously, constitutes ₹55-60 of every ₹102 litre in Delhi alone — meaning that even dramatic movements in crude oil prices affect only the non-tax portion of what you pay.

<div class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6 sm:p-7">
  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-mute">
    <span aria-hidden="true">🔍</span> The Replacement Cost Principle — And Its Selective Application
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">When crude oil prices rise, retailers and refiners justify immediate price hikes using what economists call the "replacement cost principle": even if the fuel currently in their tanks was bought at last month's lower price, they price it at what it will cost to replace it next month at the new higher rate. This sounds technically reasonable — and in a genuine free market, it is a defensible accounting position. The problem, as one economic analysis notes, is that "when oil prices drop, that same principle is forgotten with astonishing speed." The replacement cost logic applies aggressively in one direction and vanishes entirely in the other.</p>
</div>

In India specifically, the structure of the fuel pricing system adds additional layers of asymmetry. The three state-owned Oil Marketing Companies — IndianOil, BPCL, and HPCL — together control over 90% of India's 1,03,000 fuel stations. This is not a competitive retail market where price signals flow efficiently in both directions. It is a concentrated market where pricing decisions are made by three entities that, while technically competing with each other, operate under common government oversight, broadly coordinate on pricing timing, and share the same cost structure from the same upstream suppliers.

## Six Reasons Why the Feather Falls So Slowly

The rockets and feathers effect is not the result of one single factor. It is the predictable output of several forces operating simultaneously — some of them genuinely structural, some of them actively exploitative, and some of them psychological. Here are the six most important.

<div class="not-prose my-8 space-y-5 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6 sm:p-7">
  <div class="flex gap-4">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-canvas text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">1</span>
    <p class="text-sm leading-relaxed text-body"><strong class="text-ink">Inventory Accounting — The "Old Stock" Excuse.</strong> When crude prices fall, refiners and retailers argue that their existing stock was purchased at higher prices and must be sold at those higher prices first before the lower cost can flow through. This logic — while technically defensible — conveniently disappears when prices rise: no refiner waits to sell its cheaper old stock before hiking prices based on new, higher crude costs. The accounting principle is applied asymmetrically and always in the direction that benefits the seller.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="flex gap-4">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-canvas text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">2</span>
    <p class="text-sm leading-relaxed text-body"><strong class="text-ink">Market Concentration — When Everyone Charges the Same.</strong> In a truly competitive market, even one retailer cutting prices to gain market share would force all competitors to follow. But in a concentrated market — whether India's three-OMC structure or the tight oligopolies of petrol retail in most countries — price competition on the way down is muted. No single player has the individual incentive to cut prices aggressively because they know competitors may not follow, potentially leaving them with lower margins for no gain in volume in a market where demand is largely inelastic. Everyone waits. Everyone wins. The consumer loses.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="flex gap-4">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-canvas text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">3</span>
    <p class="text-sm leading-relaxed text-body"><strong class="text-ink">Search Cost Asymmetry — You Search When Prices Rise, Not When They Fall.</strong> Research by economist Matthew Lewis at UC Berkeley showed that consumer search behaviour is asymmetric: when fuel prices rise sharply, consumers actively search for cheaper options — driving an extra kilometre to find a lower-priced pump, comparing apps, changing their fuelling habits. When prices are stable or falling slowly, consumers become complacent. They do not search. This reduced search intensity removes the competitive pressure that would otherwise force faster price decreases. Retailers know consumers are not comparing prices carefully, and they respond accordingly.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="flex gap-4">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-canvas text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">4</span>
    <p class="text-sm leading-relaxed text-body"><strong class="text-ink">Margin Recovery — Recouping What Was Lost.</strong> In India's context, this is particularly significant. When crude prices were rising steeply through 2025 and early 2026, the state-owned OMCs were selling fuel below their true cost — accumulating what the industry calls "under-recoveries." When crude prices eventually ease, the OMCs do not pass on the full relief to consumers. They use the lower crude cost environment to rebuild their margins to sustainable levels. This is not always manipulation — the under-recoveries were real and the companies genuinely needed relief. But from the consumer's perspective, the math is the same: lower crude does not mean lower pump prices.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="flex gap-4">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-canvas text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">5</span>
    <p class="text-sm leading-relaxed text-body"><strong class="text-ink">Government Revenue Interests — The Silent Beneficiary.</strong> This is the most structurally important factor in India that rarely gets named directly. The central government collects excise duty on every litre of fuel sold. State governments collect VAT. When crude prices fall and the government chooses not to pass on the saving — instead maintaining current pump prices while reducing OMC under-recoveries — it is effectively converting the crude price windfall into government revenue rather than consumer relief. Neither the Centre nor the states have a direct financial incentive to cut fuel taxes when crude falls; both have strong revenue incentives to keep taxes unchanged and let the lower crude cost improve OMC balance sheets rather than consumer wallets.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="flex gap-4">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-canvas text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">6</span>
    <p class="text-sm leading-relaxed text-body"><strong class="text-ink">Inelastic Demand — You Have No Choice.</strong> Underlying all of this is the fundamental economic reality that makes fuel uniquely vulnerable to price manipulation: demand for fuel is highly inelastic. You need to commute to work regardless of whether petrol costs ₹95 or ₹115. The auto-rickshaw driver needs diesel. The truck carrying vegetables to your market needs diesel. The bus needs CNG. Unlike discretionary goods, where high prices cause consumers to buy less and force sellers to compete, fuel demand barely changes in response to price changes in the short run. This lack of consumer exit option removes the market discipline that would otherwise force faster downward price adjustments.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## The Psychological Trap: Why We Accept It Every Time

Here is the part of this story that is the most uncomfortable, because it implicates not just market structure or government policy but the predictable workings of human psychology. The rockets and feathers effect is not just a pricing strategy — it is a strategy that works specifically because of how the human brain normalises financial pain over time.

<div class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6 sm:p-7">
  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-mute">
    <span aria-hidden="true">🧠</span> The Normalisation Mechanism
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm font-bold text-ink">By the time the reason for the hike fades, the higher price feels normal.</p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">When a crisis triggers a fuel price hike, the hike is newsworthy. You notice it. You discuss it. You calculate how much more your monthly commute will cost. Your brain registers this as a change from the baseline. But as weeks pass and the crisis fades from the news cycle, the elevated price becomes your new baseline. It no longer feels like an increase — it simply feels like the price. When crude eventually falls and the government makes a token reduction of ₹2, you experience that ₹2 as a gain from your new baseline, not as an inadequate correction of the original ₹8 hike. The manipulation works precisely because of this psychological recalibration.</p>
</div>

This is the mechanism that makes rockets and feathers so durable as a strategy. A sharp, visible hike causes outrage — but outrage that is time-limited. Slow, gradual non-reduction causes no outrage at all, because the gradual drift upward has been normalised before anyone can measure it properly. The total extraction is the same; the psychological response is completely different. And the absence of outrage on the way down is what allows the next hike cycle to begin from the already-elevated baseline.

<div class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6 sm:p-7">
  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-mute">
    <span aria-hidden="true">🔥</span> The Ratchet Effect in India
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">India's fuel pricing history shows this ratchet pattern with striking clarity. Prices rise sharply during crises — the 2022 Russia-Ukraine invasion, the 2025-26 West Asia conflict — and then partially but not fully retract when the immediate trigger fades. Each cycle leaves the baseline slightly higher than before the previous crisis began. Over a decade, the cumulative effect of these partial retreats is enormous: Indian petrol prices are significantly higher in real terms than they were in 2014, even adjusting for rupee depreciation and genuine production cost changes. Part of that difference is legitimate cost reflection. Part of it is accumulated asymmetric pricing.</p>
</div>

The pattern is not subtle. Each time crude rises, the transmission to pump prices is rapid, often pre-emptive, and widely publicised with technical justification. Each time crude falls or stabilises, the benefit is distributed first to government revenue, then to OMC margin recovery, and only lastly — partially and slowly — to consumers. The consumer is always last in the queue when prices fall and first in the queue when prices rise.

## What You Can Actually Do About It

The honest answer is that as an individual consumer, your ability to fight the rockets and feathers effect is limited. Demand for fuel is inelastic by necessity for most people, and the market structure that enables asymmetric pricing is not something any individual can restructure. But there are things that are within your control — and awareness itself is the first and most important one.

**Track crude oil prices yourself.** The price of Brent crude is publicly available in real time. The Indian basket crude price is published monthly by PPAC (Petroleum Planning & Analysis Cell). When you hear a fuel price hike being justified by "global crude prices," you can now independently verify whether that justification is proportionate. When crude falls and your pump price doesn't, you now have the language — rockets and feathers — to describe what is happening and why.

**Understand the tax structure.** Approximately ₹55-60 of every ₹102 litre in Delhi is taxes. This means that even a 20% fall in crude prices — from $113 to $90 per barrel — only affects the ~₹42 non-tax portion of your pump price. The maximum pass-through to consumers from that crude fall, even if fully transmitted, would be ₹7-8/litre, not ₹20. Understanding this prevents you from being misled by the magnitude of crude movements relative to retail price changes.

**Make structural choices when possible.** Every person who switches to a metro commute, buys an EV, installs rooftop solar, or uses CNG removes themselves partially from the petrol-diesel pricing cycle. This is a long-term strategy, not an immediate fix — but it is the only genuine escape from the captive consumer trap that makes rockets and feathers sustainable as a pricing strategy.

  <p class="text-lg font-medium italic leading-relaxed text-ink">"The most revealing thing about the rockets and feathers phenomenon is that there is no need to resort to conspiracy theories to explain it. It is the predictable result of a concentrated market, a demand that cannot react, and an opacity that always benefits the same side of the counter."</p>
  <footer class="mt-4 text-sm font-bold text-mute">— Escudo Digital, April 2026</footer>
</blockquote>

The fuel pricing system in India is not entirely a conspiracy. The under-recoveries of OMCs during crude price spikes are real. The government's fiscal constraints are real. The cost structure of the supply chain is genuinely complex. But the systematic asymmetry — the rocket and the feather — is also real, it is documented, it is studied, and it operates consistently in one direction. You deserve to know that it exists. You deserve to name it when you see it. And you deserve to hold your government and your OMCs to account for the moments when the feather could fall faster and simply doesn't.

  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">
    <span aria-hidden="true">✅</span> The One Thing to Remember
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">The next time crude oil falls by 15% and your petrol price barely moves, you now know what to call it: the rockets and feathers effect. You know why it happens (replacement cost logic, market concentration, inelastic demand, government revenue interests). You know that it is not accidental. And you know that the normalisation of the higher price — the moment when the elevated baseline stops feeling like an elevation — is exactly the mechanism that makes it possible. Awareness is not enough to fix it. But it is the beginning of demanding better.</p>
