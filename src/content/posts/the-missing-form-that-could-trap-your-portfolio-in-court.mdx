<section id="the-deadline-that-regulators-kept-postponing">
      <h2 class="section-head">The Deadline That Regulators Kept Postponing</h2>
<p>Between 2022 and mid-2024, the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) found itself caught in an awkward cycle. It set a hard deadline for demat account holders and mutual fund investors to submit nomination details or formally opt out. If they failed to comply, their accounts would be frozen for debits.</p>
<p>Then the deadline approached. Brokerages and fund houses panicked. If SEBI enforced the freeze, millions of ordinary trading accounts, systematic investment plans, and folios would grind to a halt overnight. The sheer volume of non-compliant retail portfolios was staggering.</p>
<p>So SEBI blinked, extending the deadline once, twice, and eventually dropping the freeze penalty for existing mutual fund folios while keeping it optional for older demat accounts. The regulator recognized a stubborn reality: despite dozens of automated emails, push notifications, and SMS alerts, millions of Indian retail investors deliberately scrolled past the request.</p>

    <section id="the-dangerous-myth-of-the-nominee">
      <h2 class="section-head">The Dangerous Myth of the Nominee</h2>
<p>Much of the hesitation comes from a fundamental misunderstanding of what a nominee actually is.</p>
<p>Many investors believe naming a nominee is identical to writing a Will. They worry that if they name their spouse or one of their adult children, they are irrevocably signing away their ownership or inviting family disputes before they are even in the ground.</p>
<p>Indian law says the exact opposite. A nominee is not an heir. A nominee is merely a trustee or a caretaker.</p>
<p>In December 2023, the Supreme Court settled this definitively in Shakti Yezdani v. Jayanand Jayant Salgaonkar. The court ruled that nomination provisions under the Companies Act do not create a separate line of succession. A nominee holds the shares on behalf of the lawful legal heirs identified under personal succession laws or an existing Will.</p>
<p>Nomination does not decide who ultimately gets the wealth. It decides who can access the portfolio without having to spend eighteen months in a district court obtaining a succession certificate.</p>
      <p class="source-note"><em>Supreme Court of India, Shakti Yezdani &amp; Anr. v. Jayanand Jayant Salgaonkar &amp; Ors. (Civil Appeal No. 7157 of 2013, decided Dec 14, 2023).</em></p>
    </section>
    <section id="why-indian-families-prefer-silence-over-paperwork">
      <h2 class="section-head">Why Indian Families Prefer Silence Over Paperwork</h2>
<p>If the law makes nomination so straightforward, why the collective refusal to fill out the form?</p>
<p>The answer is cultural rather than technical.</p>
<p>In most Indian households, money and mortality are two subjects never brought to the same dining table. Asking an aging parent about their demat nominee feels morbid, even predatory. Similarly, parents holding shares worth lakhs or crores worry about family optics: if an investor has two children, picking one nominee feels like playing favorites, even though depositories now allow percentage-based splits across up to three nominees.</p>
<p>There is also digital inertia. Submitting a nomination online requires an Aadhaar OTP linked to a current mobile number, plus the PAN and birth dates of the nominees. For someone who opened a demat account ten years ago with a physical broker, tracking down those documents feels like a weekend chore worth postponing indefinitely.</p>
<p>The cost of that procrastination, however, does not fall on the investor. It falls squarely on the survivors.</p>
    </section>
    <section id="the-bureaucratic-black-hole-waiting-on-the-other-side">
      <h2 class="section-head">The Bureaucratic Black Hole Waiting on the Other Side</h2>
<p>Consider what happens when an investor dies without a registered nominee.</p>
<p>The depository cannot simply release the shares to a grieving family member. Even if everyone in the family agrees on who should inherit what, depositories and asset management companies are legally bound by strict transmission guidelines.</p>
<p>For portfolios exceeding nominal threshold values (often ₹5 lakh or ₹15 lakh, depending on the depository and entity), the broker demands a formal succession certificate, a probated Will, or a decree from a competent civil court. Obtaining a succession certificate in an Indian city involves hiring a lawyer, paying court fees that can reach 2% to 3% of the portfolio value, publishing public notices in local newspapers, and waiting months for court dates.</p>
<p>If the shares remain untouched for seven consecutive years and dividends go unclaimed, the law forces the company to transfer those shares to the Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF). Recovering physical shares or demat balances from the IEPF Authority is one of the most painfully drawn-out bureaucratic procedures in Indian finance, often requiring private recovery agents who pocket up to 20% of the entire portfolio just to navigate the paperwork.</p>
      <p class="source-note"><em>Investor Education and Protection Fund Authority (IEPF) transmission guidelines and Indian Succession Act, 1925.</em></p>

    <section id="what-to-do">
      <h2 class="section-head">What this means for you</h2>
<p>Open your brokerage app (Zerodha, Groww, AngelOne, or your bank broker) today. Go to profile settings, find the &#39;Nominee&#39; section, and check if one is listed. If not, add your chosen nominees, assign explicit percentage shares totaling 100%, and verify using your Aadhaar e-sign. It takes under five minutes and spares your legal heirs a multi-year legal project.</p>
    </section>
    <section id="conclusion">
      <h2 class="section-head">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Leaving money behind without registering a nominee does not preserve family harmony. It turns your life savings into a frozen asset, waiting for court registries and government recovery portals to decide who gets to unlock it. The five minutes you refuse to spend today will cost your family months of paperwork tomorrow.</p>
