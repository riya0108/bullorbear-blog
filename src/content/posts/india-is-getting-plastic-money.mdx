## 1. The Note in Your Wallet Is Falling Apart — Literally

Pull a ₹100 note from your wallet right now. Chances are, it is slightly soft at the fold, perhaps a little frayed at the corners, possibly carrying the faint smell of a hundred different hands and a hundred different transactions. That note has probably been in circulation for 18 months. The RBI is spending a staggering amount of money replacing it — and every note like it — every single year.

In the financial year ending March 2025, the Reserve Bank of India spent **₹6,372.8 crore** just on printing currency notes — a 24.9% jump from the ₹5,101.4 crore spent the previous year. That surge in cost was not because India was printing fancier notes. It was because India is printing *more* notes to replace the ones that keep wearing out. In FY25, a record **23.8 billion soiled and damaged notes** were destroyed and removed from circulation — a 12.3% increase from the 21.24 billion removed in FY24. The largest share of those destroyed notes were ₹500 notes, followed by ₹100 notes — the two most-used denominations in India's cash economy.

This is the problem that has finally pushed the RBI to revive a plan it has been sitting on for over a decade: replacing traditional cotton-based paper banknotes with polymer banknotes. And after discussions at two consecutive central board meetings — held in Patna and Mumbai in May 2026 — a pilot project announcement is expected imminently.

## 2. What Exactly Is a Polymer Banknote?

The term "plastic note" is informal but largely accurate. A polymer banknote is made from a thin, flexible synthetic substrate — typically biaxially oriented polypropylene (BOPP) — rather than the cotton-and-linen paper blend that traditional banknotes use. The material is coated with special inks and protective layers that give it a smooth, slightly glossy feel that is distinctly different from paper currency.

If you have held an Australian dollar, a British pound sterling after 2016, a Canadian dollar, a Singapore dollar, or a New Zealand dollar, you have held a polymer note. You may have noticed they feel subtly different — slightly stiffer when new, with a distinctive transparent "window" cut into the design that is one of the technology's signature anti-counterfeiting features.

The process of making polymer notes is more technically complex than cotton-paper printing, but the raw substrate material — polypropylene — is actually cheaper than the specialised cotton-based paper that most countries import for banknote printing. India currently imports a significant portion of its banknote paper, which means the cost is subject to foreign exchange fluctuations on top of the base material price. Domestically manufactured polymer substrate would eliminate that particular vulnerability.

  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">
    <span aria-hidden="true">🔬</span> How It's Made
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Polymer notes start as a thin film of clear polypropylene plastic, which is coated with a white opaque layer on both sides. The note design — including security features like holograms, micro-printing, colour-shifting inks, and transparent windows — is then printed onto this surface using specialised inks that bond with the polymer substrate. The result is a note that is chemically bonded throughout, not just printed on top of a substrate.</p>
</div>

Australia was the pioneer, introducing the world's first polymer note in 1988, and now produces polymer banknotes for dozens of other countries through a company called Note Printing Australia. The technology has had nearly 40 years of real-world testing across different climates, economic conditions, and usage patterns — and the verdict from countries that have adopted it is consistently positive on durability, security, and long-term cost.

The economic case is clearest in the durability column: a polymer note that lasts 5–7 years compared to a paper note that lasts 1.5–2 years at the same denomination means you need to print that denomination 3–4 times less often over a decade. Given that RBI spent ₹6,372 crore on printing in FY25 alone — a number that is rising every year — the long-term savings potential from polymer adoption is genuinely significant, even accounting for the higher per-note production cost.

## 4. Why Is the RBI Doing This Now, After a Decade?

This is not a new idea. The RBI first formally proposed a polymer note pilot nearly 15 years ago — and India came very close to launching it. Understanding why it stalled then, and what has changed now, explains both the caution and the renewed urgency.

## 5. The Benefits Are Real — So Are the Challenges

The countries that have made this switch successfully — Australia, Canada, the UK — each encountered implementation challenges that took years to resolve. India's context adds several layers of complexity that a straightforward cost-benefit analysis would miss.

## 6. What This Actually Means for You — the Ordinary Indian

The RBI has signalled it will begin with a phased pilot — most likely the ₹10 and ₹20 denominations — before any broader rollout. This means the transition will be gradual, not overnight, and paper notes will remain in circulation alongside polymer notes for a significant period. No one will be asked to surrender paper notes; they will simply be replaced by polymer as they wear out and are withdrawn from circulation.

For most Indians, the day-to-day experience of using currency will change minimally. You will notice the note feels different — slightly more slippery when new, with a transparent window you can see through (which many people in Australia initially used as a sort of novelty, holding the note up to light to see the security feature). Folding the note in a wallet is slightly stiffer until the note has been in circulation for a while and softens slightly with use.

For businesses that handle large volumes of cash — shopkeepers, market vendors, petrol pump operators — there will be an adjustment period with cash counting machines. For banks and their back-end currency processing centres, there will be infrastructure upgrades. The RBI has indicated that ATM dispensing compatibility has been resolved, which removes the biggest operational barrier.

One dimension that will be specific to India is the *soil and grime factor*. Indian currency passes through an extraordinary variety of hands, conditions, and contexts — from air-conditioned retail transactions to muddy agricultural markets, from humid coastal fish markets to dusty desert roadside stalls. If polymer notes perform in those conditions the way they have in Australia's varied climates and Bangladesh's tropical humidity, the case for full-scale adoption will be compelling.

## 7. Is This the Right Move? The Honest Verdict

The economics are clear. India is spending more and more to maintain a system that produces notes that degrade faster and faster. **23.8 billion notes destroyed in a single year** is not a sustainable currency management strategy. The global evidence — from Australia, Canada, the UK, and over 25 other nations — shows that polymer notes reduce long-term currency management costs, reduce counterfeiting, and improve durability in real-world conditions. India's tropical climate makes the waterproofing and moisture resistance particularly relevant.

The caution is equally justified. India's scale is unlike any other country that has made this transition. The UK switching from paper to polymer was complex; India switching from paper to polymer involves currency volumes many times larger, infrastructure spread across 700 districts, and a public that spans every imaginable level of financial literacy and digital access. The pilot-first approach is not timidity — it is sensible engineering for a system with 1.4 billion users.

What this ultimately represents is the RBI doing something it does not always get credit for: treating the management of physical currency as a long-term financial problem worth solving carefully. In an era when every policy conversation about money in India gets immediately framed as a digital payments story, the fact that India still uses billions of physical notes every day — and will for many years — makes this decision genuinely consequential.

Your next ₹10 note might feel like plastic. That is probably a good thing.
