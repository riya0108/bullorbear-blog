## 1. The Headline Number That Stopped India in Its Tracks

On the morning of June 3, 2026, India's Securities and Exchange Board issued a 109-page interim order against Rajesh Exports Limited — a Bengaluru-based company that had quietly positioned itself as India's largest gold refiner and one of its biggest companies by reported revenue. The number in that order is what made everyone stop scrolling: **₹15.15 lakh crore**.

That is the alleged revenue inflation. ₹15,15,00,00,00,00,000. Written out, it has thirteen zeroes. It is larger than India's entire defence budget for over a decade. It is larger than the GDP of most countries in the world. The Harshad Mehta scam of 1992 — the event that defined a generation of financial fraud in India — was ₹4,000 crore. If SEBI's allegations hold up under scrutiny and legal challenge, the Rajesh Exports case would be over 37,000 times larger in absolute value.

And the way it was allegedly done — using a prestigious Swiss refinery as a financial mirror, attributing third-party gold to company revenue, and keeping subsidiary accounts outside public scrutiny for years — is a masterclass in how modern corporate fraud hides in plain sight behind legitimacy, scale, and deliberate opacity.

  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">
    <span aria-hidden="true">📌</span> The Company at the Centre
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Rajesh Exports Ltd (REL) is India's largest gold jewellery manufacturer and exporter, founded by Rajesh Mehta. The company owns Valcambi SA — the world's largest precious metals refinery — acquired in 2015 for $400 million. For years, the group's sheer scale made it one of India's highest-revenue companies by turnover. That size, it now appears, may have been a key part of the problem — and possibly the design.</p>
</div>

## 2. From ₹1,200 and a Dream to a Global Gold Empire

The story of Rajesh Exports begins not with grandeur but with ₹1,200 — the borrowed capital with which Rajesh Mehta started what was initially a silver trading business in the 1980s. From those beginnings in Bengaluru, Mehta built a vertically integrated gold value chain over three decades: mining to refining to manufacturing to retail to export. By the 2010s, Rajesh Exports had the distinction of being one of the world's largest gold jewellery manufacturers by volume, winning government export awards, and operating on a scale that impressed analysts and institutions alike.

The crown jewel of Mehta's empire came in 2015, when he acquired Valcambi SA — a 60-year-old Swiss precious metals refinery headquartered in Balerna, Switzerland — for $400 million in an all-cash deal. Valcambi was not just any refinery. It was the world's largest, with a track record of processing 945 tonnes of gold and 325 tonnes of silver annually. It had processed gold for sovereign mints, central banks, and bullion banks for six decades. Acquiring it gave Rajesh Exports a level of international credibility that would prove, in retrospect, to be precisely the asset that made the alleged fraud possible.

<div class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6 sm:p-7">
  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">
    <span aria-hidden="true">🔍</span> Why Valcambi Was the Perfect Cover
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Investors and analysts who might otherwise scrutinise a holding company's numbers with forensic intensity often afford reduced scepticism when a subsidiary carries a prestigious, internationally recognised name. Valcambi was not just recognisable — it was revered in precious metals circles. When Rajesh Exports attributed its revenue to Valcambi, the brand carried enough credibility that questions were not asked with the rigour they deserved. The prestige of the Swiss brand sat like a shield between the reported numbers and genuine investigative scrutiny.</p>
</div>

## 3. What SEBI's 109-Page Order Actually Found

SEBI's interim order, signed by whole-time member Kamlesh Chandra Varshney and released on June 3, 2026, organises its findings into three interconnected categories of alleged irregularity — each more structurally significant than the last.

**The core allegation — revenue inflation through Valcambi.** SEBI says that between FY2021 and FY2025, Rajesh Exports reported consolidated revenues of approximately ₹15.18 lakh crore. Of this, a staggering ₹15.15 lakh crore — or 99.8% of the total — was attributed to overseas subsidiaries, primarily Valcambi SA and an intermediate holding company called GGR (which owns Valcambi). The problem: Valcambi's own independently audited standalone financial statements show revenues in the range of $70 million to $100 million annually. That is ₹580–830 crore per year. Against Valcambi's audited standalone figures, the consolidated number attributed to it was thousands of times larger — and the gap cannot be explained by any standard accounting treatment.

<blockquote class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-cat-money bg-surface-card p-6 dark:border-cat-money-dark sm:p-7">
  <p class="text-lg font-medium italic leading-relaxed text-ink">It is not clear as to how the consolidating entity changes fundamentals of accounting by including market value of goods belonging to third parties as its revenue, when the operating entity itself accounts for only value addition.</p>
  <footer class="mt-4 text-sm font-bold text-mute">— SEBI's 109-page interim order, June 3, 2026</footer>
</blockquote>

**Derivative trades recorded as sales.** SEBI alleges that derivative trades worth approximately ₹11,487 crore were incorrectly recorded as actual sales and purchases in the company's accounts. This is a significant allegation because it goes beyond an accounting policy dispute into territory that looks like deliberate misclassification of financial instruments — booking the notional value of derivatives as actual trading revenue to inflate top-line numbers.

**Alleged diversion into personal accounts.** Beyond the revenue figures, SEBI's preliminary findings suggest the diversion of approximately ₹339 crore into personal accounts. While this amount is smaller than the headline revenue inflation figure, it is the most serious allegation in terms of criminal implication — it crosses from accounting irregularity into what could constitute criminal misappropriation.

  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-error-deep">
    <span aria-hidden="true">⚠️</span> SEBI's Own Words
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">The regulator described the scale of the alleged irregularities as <strong class="text-ink">"egregious and unheard of"</strong> — language that SEBI does not use lightly. The order also referred the matter to NFRA (National Financial Reporting Authority) for examination of the auditors who signed off on these accounts for five consecutive years. When a regulator refers the auditors for examination, it is asking a structural question: how did professionals with legal obligations to verify these numbers sign them as accurate?</p>
</div>

## 5. Is This Really Bigger Than Harshad Mehta?

The comparison to Harshad Mehta is inevitable, but it requires careful handling. The 1992 Harshad Mehta scam was ₹4,000 crore and involved circular stock market transactions that inflated share prices using funds illegally diverted from the banking system. It shook the entire Bombay Stock Exchange, wiped out retail investor savings, and ultimately triggered sweeping reforms that created SEBI as a genuinely independent regulator. In raw numbers, yes, ₹15.15 lakh crore versus ₹4,000 crore means the Rajesh Exports allegations are — if proven — roughly 37,000 times larger.

But numbers alone do not define the severity of a financial scandal. Here is the honest comparison:

The critical distinction is this: Harshad Mehta's scam was real money actually stolen from real bank accounts and used to artificially inflate real stock prices. The Rajesh Exports case, as currently alleged, is primarily about inflated reported revenues — numbers on paper that made the company appear larger than it was, potentially allowing higher valuations, easier fundraising, and enhanced credibility. The question of how much actual money was moved improperly — as opposed to how much revenue was falsely reported — is what the ongoing forensic investigation is trying to determine.

The ₹339 crore alleged diversion into personal accounts is the cash-theft allegation. The rest is about misrepresentation of scale — which matters enormously for investor valuations, LIC's exposure, and market integrity, but is a different category of harm from the Mehta-style outright bank fund theft.</p>

## 7. The Company's Defence — What Rajesh Exports Says

Rajesh Exports has not gone silently. In a statement released shortly after SEBI's order, the company said: *"The major point misinterpreted about the revenues of the company is totally misplaced"* — arguing that its massive consolidated revenues are legitimate because they primarily reflect the operations of Valcambi, which processes enormous volumes of gold on behalf of third-party clients.

The company's accounting argument goes like this: Valcambi is a toll refiner and gold trader — it processes gold on behalf of banks, mints, and institutional clients, and in doing so, the full market value of the gold that passes through its hands gets recorded as revenue, even though the gold does not belong to Valcambi or Rajesh Exports. This is called "gross revenue" or "gross throughput" accounting — a method used by some commodity traders and processors where the full value of goods handled is counted as revenue, not just the processing fee or value addition.

This is a legitimate accounting method that exists in the industry. The problem, SEBI argues, is that Valcambi's own standalone audited accounts — which would show the same revenue if this accounting method were being applied there — show only a fraction of the consolidated figures. The gap between what Valcambi says it earned in its own books and what the consolidated accounts say it earned is precisely what SEBI calls "irreconcilable." The company's defence rests on accounting policy; SEBI's case rests on the numbers not adding up even within the company's own stated accounting framework.

<div class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6 sm:p-7">
  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">
    <span aria-hidden="true">⚖️</span> Innocent Until Proven — The Legal Reality
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">It is critically important to note that SEBI's order is an <strong class="text-ink">interim order based on preliminary findings</strong> — not a final conviction. Rajesh Mehta has been given the opportunity to respond. The forensic investigation covering FY20–FY24 is still ongoing. The company has the right to appeal and present evidence. In Indian securities law, interim orders can and do get modified or even reversed after full hearings. The allegations are extraordinarily serious, but the legal process has a long road ahead before final determinations are made.</p>
</div>

## 8. What Should Have Happened — The Systemic Failure

The most troubling dimension of this case is not Rajesh Mehta or Rajesh Exports specifically. It is the question of how a listed company can allegedly misrepresent its revenues by ₹15 lakh crore over five years, with auditors signing off, LIC increasing its stake, government departments awarding PLI scheme benefits, and SEBI allegedly taking seven months to act on the first investor complaint.

This is a systemic failure involving multiple gatekeepers simultaneously. The auditors are the first line of defence: it is their professional and legal obligation to verify that consolidated revenue figures can be reconciled with the actual accounts of the subsidiaries being consolidated. SEBI is the second line: its surveillance systems are specifically designed to detect unusual financial patterns in listed companies. LIC's investment team is the third: as a sophisticated institutional investor increasing a stake from 2% to 10.8%, it has fiduciary responsibilities to its policyholders that include asking hard questions about the numbers underpinning its investment.

The Congress party's pointed question — "Why did SEBI wait seven months to act?" — goes directly to this systemic failure. When an ordinary shareholder can identify something that looks wrong in March and SEBI only begins a formal investigation in October, something in the regulatory architecture has failed the investors it exists to protect.
