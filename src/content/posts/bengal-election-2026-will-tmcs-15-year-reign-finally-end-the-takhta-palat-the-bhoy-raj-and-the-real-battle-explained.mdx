## 15 years in power — why this election is fundamentally different

Mamata Banerjee first swept to power in 2011, ending 34 years of Left Front rule in Bengal. She won again in 2016 and, despite fierce predictions of a BJP wave, secured a crushing 215-seat majority in 2021. Every time analysts said Bengal was ready for change, the verdict went the other way. But 2026 is different — and the difference is not just a single factor but a perfect storm of accumulated grievances that even TMC's formidable welfare machinery and Mamata's personal popularity are struggling to absorb.

The word that haunts TMC's 2026 campaign is **anti-incumbency**. Fifteen years in power is a long time in any democracy, and in Bengal it means that every dissatisfied teacher, every unemployed youth, every woman who feels unsafe, every family displaced by political violence, and every voter who found their name deleted from the rolls has had more than a decade to connect their frustration to one ruling party. The BJP has been relentless in framing this election not as a contest of policies but as a referendum on TMC's fitness to govern.

## The voter list controversy: 91 lakh names gone — who lost them?

One of the most explosive issues of the 2026 campaign has nothing to do with candidates or manifestoes — it is about whether certain voters were deliberately removed from the rolls. The Election Commission's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) exercise removed approximately 91 lakh names — nearly 12% of the entire Bengal electorate — before the election. The official justification: cleansing the rolls of deceased and absentee voters. The TMC, in a reversal of its usual position, cried foul. Observers noted that roughly 65% of the affected voters were from Muslim communities, and Dalit Hindus — particularly from the Matua community — were also heavily affected in certain districts. The BJP defended the exercise as the purging of bogus entries of illegal migrants. For millions of voters, it became a deeply personal issue: standing in line at a polling booth only to find that you no longer officially exist.

## Why TMC is not as confident as it used to be

In 2016 and 2021, TMC entered election season with the swagger of a party that knew it would win. Mamata campaigned from a wheelchair after being "injured" in Nandigram in 2021, building herself into a symbol of martyrdom and defiance. She won by 213 seats. In 2026, the optics are different. Mamata has been publicly alleging that the BJP is trying to bribe her candidates to defect. She has questioned the heavy deployment of central forces, implying they are being used to help the BJP rather than ensure free and fair voting. She has called the SIR exercise a "conspiracy to disenfranchise real voters." These are not the words of a confident ruling party — they are the words of a party that knows its margins have shrunk.

In the 2024 Lok Sabha elections — a useful mid-term indicator — TMC won 29 of West Bengal's 42 seats, but the BJP held 12. More significantly, the vote share gap between the two parties narrowed considerably compared to 2021. Districts like North Bengal, parts of Murshidabad, and the Junglemahal region in West Midnapore showed genuine swing. The anti-incumbency sentiment that Mamata successfully deflected in 2021 by framing the election as "Bengal vs Delhi" has been reignited by a series of events — RG Kar, Sandeshkhali, the teacher scam — that are impossible to frame as central government conspiracies because the victims are ordinary Bengalis and the accused are TMC leaders.

## Phase 1: what happened today and what it signals

Voting in Phase 1 covered 152 of 294 constituencies across 16 districts, including all of North Bengal and key seats in Murshidabad, Birbhum, Nadia, and Hooghly. Turnout hit nearly 90% by 5 PM — with the Dakshin Dinajpur district leading at 93.12% — and was expected to cross 95% by close of polls. On the surface, high turnout benefits challengers in most democracies, as it typically indicates motivated opposition voters coming out in force. But in Bengal, where TMC has historically been very effective at mobilising its own vote, high turnout is not straightforwardly bad for the ruling party.

What is more telling is the nature of the complaints: over 500 formal complaints were received by the Election Commission by noon alone, with another 375 through the cVIGIL app. BJP polling agents were allegedly assaulted in Birbhum's Labhpur and Malda's Chanchal. In Birbhum's Khoyrasol, voters alleged that votes cast for TMC were being registered for BJP — a claim that sparked protests and a temporary polling halt. In Domkal, voters were allegedly prevented from reaching booths and required security escort. These incidents reveal a deeply polarised ground — and a state where every vote is genuinely contested.

## The Morgan Stanley question Bengal's voters are really answering

Strip away the noise — the EVM controversies, the voter list fights, the identity rhetoric — and what this election is really asking is a simple question that every democracy eventually forces its people to answer: is the fear of change greater than the frustration with continuity? Bengal's voters have answered this question four times in succession by choosing continuity. In 2011, they chose change after 34 Left years. Since then, they have consistently backed Mamata — even as the scandals mounted — because the alternative felt abstract and threatening.

What 2026 has done is make the cost of continuity concrete: a doctor raped and murdered inside a government hospital. A TMC strongman running from the law for 55 days while women in his constituency could not speak. Fifty crore rupees in cash stacked in a flat connected to the Education Minister. Voting booths where your name may no longer exist. These are not abstract policy failures. They are personal stories — and they travel faster and farther than welfare scheme receipts.

## The bottom line

Bengal 2026 is not like 2016 or 2021. It is the most consequential election this state has held since Mamata came to power, because for the first time, the weight of 15 years of governance — with all its welfare triumphs and all its spectacular failures of accountability — is being tested in a single vote. The takhta palat may or may not happen on May 4. But what is certain is that something in Bengal has already changed: the silence around the Bhoy Raj has broken, and the voters who were afraid to speak loudly in 2021 have been given four years of additional reasons to vote differently in 2026. Whether they do — and whether they can do so freely — is the question this election is trying, imperfectly, to answer.
