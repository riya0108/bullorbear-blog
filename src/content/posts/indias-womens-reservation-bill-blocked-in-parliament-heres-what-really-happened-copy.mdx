*A historic bill meant to guarantee one-third of parliamentary seats for women collapsed in the Lok Sabha on April 18 — not because anyone opposes women in politics, but because of a deeply divisive map-redrawing fight between India's north and south.*

The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 needed two-thirds of the MPs present and voting to pass — a threshold of 352 votes. The government fell 54 votes short, marking the first major legislative defeat for Prime Minister Narendra Modi's government in 12 years. PM Modi was present in the House when the vote was announced.

### What exactly was this bill trying to do?

The story begins in September 2023, when Parliament unanimously passed the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam — the 106th Constitutional Amendment — reserving one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies for women. It was a landmark moment, cheered across party lines. But there was a catch: the law would only come into force after a new census was conducted and a fresh delimitation exercise — a redrawing of constituency boundaries — was completed. In short, women's reservation was passed but not yet active.

Fast forward to April 2026. The Modi government called a three-day special session of Parliament to table three interconnected bills: expand the Lok Sabha from 543 to 850 seats, allow delimitation to be based on the 2011 census (instead of waiting for the upcoming 2026–27 census), and in doing so, finally trigger women's reservation. The government even notified the 2023 Act on April 16, officially bringing it into force — but the real activation still depended on the new delimitation happening first.

### So why did the opposition vote it down?

The short answer: the north-south divide. India's constituency boundaries have been frozen since 1971. States like Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Rajasthan have seen massive population growth since then, while southern states like Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and Karnataka implemented population control programmes effectively, and their populations grew far more slowly. If seats are now redistributed based on population, the south gets punished for doing exactly what the Centre once encouraged.

Based on 2011 Census estimates, Tamil Nadu stood to lose around 7 seats (from 39 to 32), while Kerala could lose 5 seats (from 20 to 15). Meanwhile, Uttar Pradesh would gain 9 seats, and Bihar would gain more. For southern states, this isn't an abstraction — it is a direct reduction in their parliamentary voice and federal clout. Tamil Nadu Chief Minister M.K. Stalin and the DMK party led the charge against the bills, with southern states rallying together in rare cross-party solidarity.

### Was women's reservation the real agenda — or a shield?

Critics across the political spectrum argued that while women's representation was the public face of the bill, the real substance was a structural reshaping of India's electoral map that would benefit BJP strongholds in the north. Political scientist Yogendra Yadav put it bluntly: the women's quota could have been implemented through a simple amendment, but it was instead bundled with the contentious delimitation exercise. Opposition parties, including Congress, insisted they were fully in favour of women's reservation — just not tied to this particular redrawing of boundaries.

The Congress party proposed an alternative: implement women's reservation unconditionally and immediately, expedite the new census, and build a separate national consensus on delimitation rather than rushing through all three in a three-day special session. The government, however, maintained that the two were inseparable — that true women's representation required new constituencies to first be properly drawn.

### What happens now?

With the constitutional amendment defeated, the government immediately withdrew the remaining two bills — the Delimitation Commission Bill and the Union Territories Amendment Bill — rather than face further votes. The Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam technically remains in force since its notification on April 16, but without the accompanying delimitation, the 33% quota cannot actually be implemented. Women in India are no closer to guaranteed representation in Parliament today than they were yesterday.

The bill's failure is, at its core, a story about competing priorities: a genuine desire to advance gender equity, a government accused of wielding that equity as political cover, and a set of southern states unwilling to trade their federal voice for it. Until there is a genuine consensus on how India's electoral map should be redrawn — and who wins and loses in that process — women's reservation will remain the most popular promise that Indian politics never seems to keep.
