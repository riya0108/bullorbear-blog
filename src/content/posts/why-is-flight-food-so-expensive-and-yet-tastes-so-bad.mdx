You've been there. Somewhere over the Arabian Sea, thirty-five thousand feet in the air, a flight attendant places a foil-wrapped tray on your fold-out table. You peel it back with cautious optimism. Rubbery chicken. Overcooked rice that has the consistency of wet sand. A bread roll that could be used as construction material. A tiny tub of dessert that you eat anyway because you're bored and desperate. And then the bill — because you foolishly booked a low-cost carrier that doesn't include meals — shows ₹450 for a "snack box" that contains a small packet of chips and something that claims to be a sandwich.

You are angry. You are confused. You are convinced that airlines are running the world's most brazen food scam while simultaneously employing the world's worst chefs.

Here's the truth: they kind of are running a scam, and the chefs are probably fine. The full story involves physics, neuroscience, airport economics, military-grade logistics, and a very specific phenomenon that happens to your nose at altitude. Once you understand all of it, you'll still be annoyed — but at least you'll be annoyed correctly.

---

## Part 1: Why It Costs So Much — The Economics of Eating at 35,000 Feet

### You Walked Through a One-Way Gate

The single biggest driver of high airport and in-flight food prices is something economists have a precise term for: the captive consumer. The moment you pass through airport security, you have entered an economic zone unlike any other in the world. You cannot leave to find a cheaper option. You cannot order from Swiggy. You cannot walk to the dhaba down the road. Your alternatives are exactly what the terminal chooses to offer you, at whatever price those vendors decide to charge.

This is not an accident of design — it is the design. Airports deliberately locate food and retail vendors beyond security, knowing that passengers are committed and have no exit option. In economics, this is called a "captive market," and it is one of the most reliable pricing environments that exists. Standard market competition requires that a customer be able to walk away and choose a competitor. In a post-security terminal, that ability is essentially absent. Airport vendors exploit this systematically, and they're entirely within their rights to do so — it just means you pay ₹250 for a mediocre coffee that costs ₹80 outside the terminal.

### The Rent Is Astronomical

Airport real estate is among the most expensive commercial space in any country. Airport authorities charge vendors lease rates and revenue-sharing agreements that would make a high-street mall landlord blush. A food vendor at Indira Gandhi International Airport's Terminal 3 pays not just rent but also a percentage of gross revenue back to the airport authority, licensing fees, security compliance costs, and premium charges for the right to operate in a "high footfall zone." These costs are passed, entirely and unsentimental ly, to the person buying the ₹350 bottle of water.

The economics are remarkably transparent once you know what you're looking at. Airport operators generate enormous revenues from their commercial tenants — MIAL (Mumbai International Airport Limited) and Delhi International Airport Limited derive significant portions of their non-aeronautical revenue from food, beverages, and retail. The passenger is the revenue source, and the captive nature of that revenue source allows pricing that would be commercially impossible in any normally functioning market.

### The Catering Operation Is a Logistics Marvel — and a Cost Nightmare

Here is the part that most people don't think about: the food on your flight was not made on the plane. It was made in a large industrial kitchen, typically several kilometres away from the airport, by a specialised catering company. The two giants of this industry globally are Gate Gourmet and LSG Sky Chefs (now Newrest in many markets), and between them they produce hundreds of millions of meals annually for airlines worldwide.

The in-flight catering market topped $19.6 billion in 2024, and experts project it will reach approximately $35.1 billion by 2034 — growing at 6% annually. This is a massive industrial operation, and every step of it costs money that ultimately flows back to your tray.

The process works like this: meals are cooked in large batches in certified aviation catering kitchens (which must meet food safety standards stricter than a restaurant, because there is no hospital nearby if someone gets food poisoning at 37,000 feet). The cooked food is then rapidly chilled to strict temperature bands — not frozen for most meals, but held at precisely controlled refrigeration levels to prevent bacterial growth while preserving texture. It is then loaded into specialised trolleys and transferred via high-lift catering trucks — enormous, expensive vehicles with elevating platforms — directly to the aircraft galley.

An economy class meal costs the airline between $5 and $15 to produce; a business class meal runs $30–$50; and a four-course first-class meal can exceed $100 per person.

What you actually pay when you buy a meal on a low-cost carrier or at an airport terminal bears no relationship to what it cost the airline or the kitchen to produce. You are paying for the logistics chain, the security compliance, the equipment depreciation, the captive market premium, and the airport authority's revenue share — all wrapped in one foil tray.

### The Weight Tax Nobody Talks About

Here is a hidden cost that even most frequent flyers don't consciously register: everything on a plane costs fuel to carry. Heavier aircraft burn more fuel. This means that every meal, every drink trolley, every piece of crockery, every piece of galley equipment adds to the aircraft's operating fuel cost for that flight. Airlines have been obsessive about this for decades — British Airways once reportedly calculated the fuel cost of removing a single olive from its first-class salad (saving tens of thousands of dollars annually across its fleet). Meal planning, portion sizes, packaging materials, and service ware are all optimised for weight as much as for flavour and passenger satisfaction. The tray, the foil, the plastic cutlery, the miniature condiment packets — all of it is engineered to be as light as possible. That engineering costs money too.

## Part 2: Why It Tastes Bad — The Science of Eating at Altitude

Now for the part that is genuinely not the airline's (or the chef's) fault. Or at least, not entirely.
(You can also read: [The Science Behind Aeroplane Food)](https://petergreenberg.com/2016/05/26/science-behind-airplane-food/)

### Your Nose Is Essentially Broken at 37,000 Feet

Taste is not just what happens on your tongue. It is a multisensory experience in which smell — specifically the ability to detect airborne aroma molecules drifting upward through the back of your throat to your olfactory receptors — contributes an enormous amount to what you perceive as "flavour." This is why, when you have a blocked nose from a cold, even your favourite food tastes like cardboard. Your tongue can only distinguish five basic categories (sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami), but your nose can distinguish thousands of nuances. Together, they create what you experience as the complex flavour of a good meal.

In an aircraft cabin, your nose is under assault from multiple directions simultaneously.

At 30,000 feet, cabin humidity measures at around 12% — considerably drier than that of most deserts. This extraordinary dryness dehydrates your nasal mucus membranes — the moist lining of your nasal cavity through which airborne aroma molecules must pass to reach your olfactory receptors. When that lining dries out, it becomes far less efficient at capturing and transporting those molecules. You lose a significant fraction of your ability to smell, and therefore to taste.

In addition to reduced taste bud sensitivity, cabin pressurisation causes the mucus membranes to swell — as one professor at the University of Colorado School of Medicine put it, "Cabin pressure decreases the volatility of odour molecules, or their ability to vaporise and enter the nose." Your nasal cavity swells and blocks the channel through which flavour aromas travel. It is almost exactly like having a mild head cold — which, as anyone who has eaten while sick knows, is a remarkably effective way to make even excellent food taste like nothing.

### Your Taste Buds Are Numbed — By 30%

Beyond the nose, the cabin environment directly suppresses your taste receptors. A landmark 2010 study commissioned by Lufthansa and conducted by the Fraunhofer Institute in Germany found that sensitivity to sweet and salty foods drops by about 30% in the air compared to the ground. German researchers conducted taste tests at sea level and in pressurised conditions equivalent to 8,000 feet of altitude — the standard cabin pressure most commercial aircraft maintain — and found that the combination of low pressure and dry air genuinely numbed taste bud sensitivity.

This is why airline food tastes bland even when the catering company has prepared it competently. The food is not under-seasoned — it is often deliberately over-seasoned to compensate. For food to taste the same in the air as it does on the ground, airline caterers must add up to 30% more sugar or salt to a meal. This is why, if you've ever eaten an airline meal at sea level — say, from one of those airport terminal grab-and-go options — it tastes oddly intense and over-salted. It was seasoned for your numb in-flight palate, not your ground-level one.

The flavours that suffer most are sweetness and saltiness. The flavours that are relatively unaffected — and sometimes even amplified at altitude — are umami (the savoury depth in things like tomato juice, soy sauce, or aged cheese) and bitterness. This is the scientific explanation for a well-known flight phenomenon that has puzzled travellers for decades: why do so many people order tomato juice on planes when they would never touch the stuff on the ground? It is not psychological. It is physiological. Tomato juice is rich in umami compounds that are relatively unaffected by altitude, making it one of the few beverages that genuinely tastes better in the air — which is why Lufthansa reportedly sells more tomato juice in-flight than beer.

### The Reheat Problem Is Real and Unavoidable

Everything you eat on a plane — with the exception of some fresh items served in first class — has been cooked on the ground, chilled, and then reheated in a convection oven roughly the size of a large microwave, powered by the aircraft's electrical system.

<blockquote class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-cat-lifestyle bg-surface-card p-6 dark:border-cat-lifestyle-dark sm:p-7">
  <p class="text-lg font-medium italic leading-relaxed text-ink">Meals are prepared in advance, so they are shelf-stable for a number of hours — then reheated in less than ideal conditions, which contributes to it not tasting great.</p>
  <footer class="mt-4 text-sm font-bold text-mute">— Charles Spence, Professor of Experimental Psychology, Oxford</footer>
</blockquote>

The reheating process is brutal to food texture. Proteins in meat contract further when reheated, squeezing out moisture and creating that characteristic rubbery quality. Starches absorb moisture during the first cooking, then release and reabsorb it during reheating in ways that make pasta gluey and rice sticky. Any component that was crispy — bread, pastry, anything fried — absorbs steam from adjacent components during the pressurised cabin atmosphere and goes limp. Vegetables, already partially cooked in the initial preparation, overcook again during reheating and become either mushy or fibrous depending on the vegetable's structure.

Airlines that invest heavily in first and business class catering work around this by selecting dishes that are either inherently suited to reheating (braises, curries, slow-cooked proteins) or by designing the preparation specifically for the reheat step — stopping the initial cooking at a point where the reheat will bring it to ideal rather than overdone. Economy class catering, produced at a fraction of the cost, cannot justify this level of recipe engineering at scale.

### The Engine Is Literally Interfering With Your Brain

This one sounds like it shouldn't be real, but it is. A 2010 study from the Fraunhofer Institute found that the constant, loud background noise of aircraft engines specifically suppresses the taste of sweet foods, while simultaneously enhancing the perception of crunchiness. The mechanism involves how the brain integrates competing sensory inputs: when one dominant sensory channel (hearing) is overwhelmed by constant loud noise, the brain allocates less processing bandwidth to weaker signals from other channels, including taste and smell. It is neurological multitasking failure, and you are losing the flavour competition to the sound of two CFM56 turbofan engines running at cruise power.

Recent neuroscience research using brain imaging has confirmed that airplane cabin noise disrupts visual perception and attentional processing of in-flight food stimuli, impacting how the brain integrates sensory information. You are not just tasting with your tongue and nose — you are tasting with your entire sensory system, including your ears. At 35,000 feet, that system is being overwhelmed.

## The Honest Conclusion: It Was You All Along. Mostly.

Here is the synthesis of everything above: if you somehow teleported an identically prepared meal from a decent restaurant kitchen into your hands at 35,000 feet, it would taste about 30% blander than it did in the restaurant, slightly more rubbery if it involved protein, and slightly less sweet than you expected. This would happen even if the chef, the ingredients, the seasoning, and the preparation were all perfect. The aircraft environment would do this to the food regardless.

The airline and the airport are still running a captive market operation that extracts above-market prices from passengers with no alternatives — that part is entirely legitimate to be annoyed about. The $6 economy meal cost that gets sold to you for ₹500 involves a mark-up that is hard to justify on any basis other than "you have nowhere else to go." That is a genuine economic exploitation of your situation.

But the taste problem? That is physics, chemistry, and neuroscience. Your nose dries out. Your taste buds lose a third of their sensitivity. Your brain's sensory processing is drowned out by engine noise. No chef, however talented, can fully overcome all of that in a convection oven at 11,000 metres.

So next time you unwrap that foil tray and grimace: keep at least half your frustration directed at the airport pricing model, acknowledge the rest is your own altitude-compromised palate, and order the tomato juice. Science says it's the one thing that genuinely tastes better up here.
