A ₹24,900 Swiss watch and a ₹799 imitation, sitting side by side. Most people genuinely can't tell which is which — and that gap has quietly become a $467 billion industry.

Picture two watches. Same silhouette, same waffle-textured dial, same integrated steel bracelet. One is a genuine Tissot PRX — the Swiss watch that went viral a few years ago for looking like a luxury sports watch ten times its price. The other is what the trade politely calls a "first copy," bought off an Instagram ad. One weighs 84 grams on the wrist and its caseback text feels engraved under a fingernail. The other weighs 71 grams and its caseback text feels flat. Before you read on, take a guess.

The 84g watch is the genuine Tissot PRX. The lighter one is one of thousands of "first copy" PRX watches sold across Instagram and WhatsApp catalogues every month. If you picked correctly, you're already better at this than most people. If you didn't, don't feel bad — that's rather the point of starting here. The Tissot PRX has been called one of the most counterfeited Swiss watches of the decade, precisely because it's photogenic enough to fool a scroll and cheap enough, as a fake, that almost anyone can afford to be fooled.

## A whole vocabulary for "not quite real"

The industry behind that ₹799 watch has an entire lexicon, depending on which rung of the quality ladder it sits on — and increasingly, that lexicon shows up in the comment sections of Instagram reels and YouTube shorts, not just old-school trade catalogues. A **master copy** (sometimes "super copy") aims to be nearly indistinguishable from the original, built with premium substitute materials and priced high, as fakes go. A **first copy** is the mainstream tier: convincing at a glance, cheaper underneath, priced at a fraction of the original. Below that sit **second copies**, and the graded replicas sold under labels like **7A, 10A, or AAA** — grading language borrowed from the electronics grey market, with no actual authority certifying any of it. It's marketing, not a standard.

The global market for fake watches specifically was valued at $5.1 billion in 2025 by the Swiss Watch Federation. And in India, apparel and consumer electronics are the most commonly faked categories — with social media ads having overtaken physical markets as the biggest single channel selling them.

## Is any of this actually legal?

Here's the part most buyers skip past: this isn't a legal grey area. Under India's Trade Marks Act, 1999, manufacturing or selling goods bearing a counterfeit trademark is a criminal offence, not just a civil dispute — **cognizable and non-bailable**, meaning police can arrest without a warrant. Sections 103 to 105 set the punishment at six months to three years in prison, plus a fine between ₹50,000 and ₹2 lakh, with steeper penalties on a second conviction. The Customs Act, 1962 lets border officials seize suspected fakes on the way into the country. A FICCI CASCADE study put the economic damage from counterfeiting and smuggling at over ₹1 lakh crore across just seven manufacturing sectors in India alone.

Buyers carry far less legal exposure than sellers — enforcement almost always targets whoever is running the counterfeit operation, not the person who bought one pair of "first copy" sneakers off Instagram. But "rarely prosecuted" is a different sentence from "legal," and it's worth knowing which one is actually true before you screenshot a seller's catalogue.

## How a first copy actually gets made

The manufacturing side is less mysterious than people assume. Many first-copy workshops sit inside the very same industrial clusters that manufacture genuine goods for global brands — sometimes it's an unauthorised extra shift on the same line, using leftover material and molds; other times it's a separate operation that reverse-engineers a product from photographs and a pair of digital calipers. Markets like Delhi's Palika Bazaar or Mumbai's Heera Panna have openly done this for decades, well-known enough to appear in tourist guides alongside the city's genuine shopping streets. The corners get cut exactly where they're least visible: full-grain leather becomes Rexine or PU, a Swiss movement becomes a generic quartz module, real gold plating becomes a thin gold-tone spray that wears through within months. In a photograph, the gap has nearly closed. In your hand, it never really has.

Whichever you picked, you're in good company either way. In global research from the International Trademark Association, **income and personal morals** came out as the two biggest factors behind this exact decision — and for most people, it's genuinely both at once. One respondent summed it up simply: buying fakes felt ethically wrong, but the price gap on the originals felt unbridgeable. In India specifically, only **36%** of counterfeit buyers say price was their main reason for buying — most point instead to access, convenience, and, quite often, simply not knowing.

None of this means you're powerless at the point of sale. Across watches, shoes, sunglasses, and clothing, the same tells repeat themselves often enough that a short, deliberate check catches most fakes before checkout.

No single signal proves anything on its own — but two or three of them disagreeing at once is a very reliable pattern.

## So — should you actually buy one?

The honest answer depends on what you're buying, and from whom. A ₹700 pair of "first copy" sunglasses is a real, unglamorous risk — cheap lenses often skip UV filtering entirely, which means your pupils dilate in the shade the tinted lenses provide while the sun does more damage to your eyes than wearing no sunglasses at all. A first-copy jacket that flakes apart in eight months isn't actually cheaper than a genuine one that lasts eight years — it's just cheaper today. And every purchase, invisible as it feels, funds a supply chain that courts and researchers have repeatedly linked to unregulated labour and, in the worst-documented cases, organised crime.

<PostCallout variant="quote">
None of that erases the real economics on the other side. A ₹2,00,000 watch is, for the overwhelming majority of people on this planet, not a rational purchase — it's an aspirational one, and aspiration doesn't pay rent.
</PostCallout>

The honest middle ground most people actually land on isn't "always buy original" or "fakes are fine, who cares" — it's simply buying with open eyes. Knowing what you're actually holding. Knowing it's illegal to sell, even if buying carries less personal risk. And not mistaking a ₹799 watch for a ₹24,900 one just because a seller's photographs happened to look convincing.

Go back to the two watches at the top of this page. Once you know what you're actually looking for — the weight in your palm, the depth of an engraving under a fingernail, the ₹24,000 gap hiding behind "too good to be true" — the difference stops being invisible. It was never really about being clever enough to spot a good fake. It's about how rarely anyone actually stops to look.
