That’s exactly what is happening in India today.

*Thousands of factory workers in Noida took to the streets, torched vehicles, and faced tear gas — not because of a local political dispute, but because a war 3,000 kilometres away has made it impossible for them to make ends meet.*

Let’s understand how.

#### How a distant war reached a factory floor in Noida

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel launched coordinated airstrikes on Iran under Operation Epic Fury, targeting military facilities and nuclear sites. Iran retaliated swiftly — and in the most economically devastating way possible. It shut down the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow chokepoint through which roughly 20% of the world's oil and a significant share of global LNG supplies flow every single day. The International Energy Agency called it the "greatest global energy security challenge in history."

#### What exploded in Noida — and why

Noida, a satellite city in Uttar Pradesh bordering Delhi, is one of Asia's largest planned industrial townships, housing thousands of manufacturing units across garments, electronics, hosiery, auto components, and more. Its factory floors are largely powered by migrant workers — many of them from Bihar, Uttar Pradesh's poorer districts, West Bengal, and Jharkhand — earning between Rs 10,000 and Rs 15,000 a month. For these workers, a sudden surge in LPG prices, grocery bills, and transport costs is not an abstraction. It is the difference between eating adequately and not.

Protests began inside individual factories and quickly spilled into the streets. By the fourth consecutive day of unrest — April 13 — the protests had turned violent. Vehicles were set on fire, stones were pelted at police, and demonstrators tried to break through barricades. Police responded with tear gas, with Gautam Budh Nagar police issuing a statement saying "minimum force" was used. Over 300 workers and labour activists were detained in Noida alone. Section 144, which prohibits assembly, was imposed in several pockets of the city, and paramilitary units were deployed. Traffic across Delhi and the NCR was paralysed for much of the day.

#### The demands: simple, urgent, and long overdue

The workers' demands were not radical — they were basic. Fixed duty hours, overtime pay at double rates, wages paid by the 10th of each month, weekly days off, and access to social security. Trade union leader Siddhanth Raj pointed out that workers had been expecting an improvement from April 1, when new labour codes were meant to come into force. Those improvements never materialised, which became the spark. Samajwadi Party president Akhilesh Yadav squarely blamed the BJP-led Uttar Pradesh government, saying, "The administration and the government are fully responsible for what happened in Noida." The state government, led by Chief Minister Yogi Adityanath, responded by suggesting a "larger conspiracy" behind the unrest — a framing that trade unions flatly rejected as a deflection from legitimate economic grievances.

Local officials in Noida did announce some concessions — doubling overtime pay, ensuring weekly offs, and guaranteeing prompt wage payment — but these were announced on Sunday, a day before the biggest explosion of unrest on Monday. Workers clearly felt it was too little, too late.

#### This is bigger than Noida

The Noida protests are the most visible flashpoint of a much wider movement. Similar unrest has been reported in the refinery belt of Barauni, industrial zones in Panipat, textile hubs in Surat, manufacturing clusters in Manesar and Singrauli, and across the NCR's Faridabad corridor. In each location, the core demands are identical: higher wages to cope with a cost of living that has outpaced earnings for years — and is now being pushed even further beyond reach by a war none of these workers voted for.

India's government has tried to cushion the blow: the Indian Petroleum Minister confirmed that the government has absorbed more than half of the fuel price increase under a federal subsidy scheme. But for workers already earning at the margin, even a partial pass-through of global price increases is catastrophic. The Reserve Bank of India is simultaneously battling a weakening rupee, rising import inflation, and growing pressure on its fiscal space — all while trying to avoid tightening policy too aggressively in an already stressed economy.

#### The bigger picture: who really pays for war?

The 2026 Iran war is the starkest illustration yet of how geopolitical conflicts thousands of kilometres away land disproportionately on the shoulders of people who have the least power to absorb them. The World Economic Forum's Global Risks Report 2026 warned explicitly that asymmetric energy shocks disproportionately burden import-dependent Asian economies. India's workers in the NCR industrial belt are living that warning in real time — in insufficient wages, in queues for LPG cylinders, in police batons and tear gas on the streets of Noida.

What began as a labour dispute has become something more: a signal that India's working class is drawing a direct line between global geopolitics, government inaction, and their own survival. Whether the authorities in Uttar Pradesh — and New Delhi — choose to hear that signal as a law-and-order problem or as an economic emergency will define what comes next.

#### Final Thought

The protests around Delhi are not just a labour issue — they are a powerful reminder of how interconnected the world has become.
