*The ₹100 note sitting in your wallet today will not buy the same things five years from now. Nobody stole from you. No one cheated you. It is simply a quiet, invisible process that has been running since currencies were invented — and understanding it might be the most important financial lesson you never got in school.*

## What Does "Money Losing Value" Actually Mean?

Money doesn't rot, burn, or disappear from your wallet. What actually happens is far more subtle: the *things* money can buy get more expensive over time, which means each unit of currency — each rupee, each dollar — commands less and less with every passing year. Economists call this a decline in **purchasing power**. Inflation is the rate at which this erosion happens. It is not an accident or a fluke. It is a structural feature of every modern economy on Earth, built in by design and managed (with varying success) by central banks and governments.

<div class="not-prose my-8 flex flex-col gap-4">
  <div class="rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <p class="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-mute">Reason 1 — Money Supply Expansion</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">More Money Chasing the Same Goods</p>
    <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">When a government or central bank creates more money — by printing notes or, more commonly today, by expanding credit — the total amount of money in circulation increases. But the number of goods and services available doesn't automatically grow at the same pace. The result is straightforward: more money competing for the same basket of goods drives prices up. Around 20% of all US dollars in circulation were created in 2020 alone during the COVID-19 pandemic stimulus — and the inflation surge that followed in 2021-22 was directly linked to this expansion.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <p class="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-mute">Reason 2 — Demand-Pull Inflation</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">Too Much Demand, Too Little Supply</p>
    <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Sometimes inflation is not caused by money printing but by an economy simply getting too hot. When employment is high, people earn more and spend more. Businesses struggle to keep up. Prices rise because demand outstrips what can be produced. This is called demand-pull inflation, and it is what central banks try to cool down by raising interest rates — making borrowing more expensive slows down spending and reduces pressure on prices.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <p class="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-mute">Reason 3 — Cost-Push Inflation</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">When Production Gets More Expensive</p>
    <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Inflation also fires up when the cost of making things rises sharply. Oil is the classic example. When crude prices spike — as they did when Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz in early 2026, pushing Brent crude from $67 to a peak of $126 per barrel — the cost of manufacturing, transporting, and packaging virtually everything goes up. Those higher costs get passed on to consumers as higher prices. Workers then demand higher wages to cope. Those higher wages raise costs further. This self-reinforcing cycle is called a wage-price spiral, and it is one of the most stubborn forms of inflation to break.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <p class="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-mute">Reason 4 — Inflation Expectations</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">What People Believe Becomes What Happens</p>
    <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">One of the most fascinating and counterintuitive causes of inflation is pure psychology. If workers and businesses believe prices will rise by 8% next year, workers demand an 8% wage hike today, and businesses raise their prices by 8% pre-emptively. The expected inflation becomes actual inflation without a single underlying economic condition changing. This is why central banks obsessively manage public communication about their inflation targets — because keeping expectations anchored at 2–3% is literally a tool for keeping inflation at 2–3%.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <p class="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-mute">Reason 5 — Government Debt &amp; Deficit Spending</p>
    <p class="mt-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">When Governments Spend More Than They Earn</p>
    <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">When governments spend more than they collect in taxes, they borrow. Large sustained deficits can stoke inflation in two ways: first, by injecting more money into the economy through spending; and second, by creating pressure to inflate away the real value of the debt over time. A government that owes ₹100 trillion in fixed-rate bonds actually benefits from inflation, because it ends up repaying those bonds with currency that is worth less in real terms. Research shows that a permanent 1% of GDP increase in a primary deficit leads, after five years, to inflationary pressure equivalent to a $300–$1,250 loss in household purchasing power per family (in 2024 dollars).</p>
  </div>
</div>

## The Gold Standard: What Ended It and Why It Matters

For most of history, currencies were tethered to a physical commodity — usually gold. A government could only print as many notes as it held in gold reserves, which naturally limited money supply expansion. In 1971, US President Richard Nixon severed the dollar's link to gold entirely, making it a *fiat currency* — backed only by the government's promise and the public's trust. From that point, money supply could grow without limit, and inflation accelerated. Every major currency in the world today is a fiat currency. The gold standard era was far from perfect, but its abolition directly enabled the scale of money creation we see today.

## The End: Hyperinflation

When inflation breaks free of all controls, it becomes hyperinflation — defined as price increases exceeding 1,000% per year.

## Is Some Inflation Actually Good?

Counter-intuitively, yes — in moderation. Most developed nations deliberately target an inflation rate of around 2–3% per year. A small amount of inflation encourages people to spend and invest rather than hoard cash, which keeps economic activity flowing. The real danger is deflation — falling prices — which sounds beneficial but is economically devastating. If you expect that TV to be cheaper next month, you wait. Everyone waits. Businesses earn less. They cut jobs. People spend even less. This deflationary spiral is what crippled the Great Depression of the 1930s, and it is why central banks are more afraid of deflation than moderate inflation.

## What Can You Actually Do About It?

You cannot stop inflation. But you can make sure your money grows faster than it erodes. The biggest mistake is leaving large amounts of cash sitting idle — at 4–5% annual inflation, ₹1 lakh in cash loses roughly ₹4,000–5,000 of real value every single year, silently, invisibly. Money losing value over time is not a conspiracy, a government failure, or a temporary blip. It is a structural feature of the modern monetary system — baked in by design and driven by a combination of money supply growth, demand pressures, supply shocks, psychology, and government fiscal choices. The only way to fight back is to understand exactly what is happening — and to make sure every rupee you own is either working for you or quietly eroding while you look the other way.
