A 22-year-old asked a stranger to shut a train door so the rain wouldn't come in. He was stabbed in the chest and abdomen for it. He died before the train reached the next station. *That sentence shouldn't exist. And yet, this year, it does — twice.*

Mayank Lohar was 22. He worked at Mumbai airport's cargo complex. On a rain-soaked evening as the monsoon arrived in the city, he asked a co-passenger to keep the train door closed so the water wouldn't drench the compartment. The other man wanted it open. Words were exchanged. Then a sharp weapon was pulled out, and within minutes, a young man was on the floor of a moving local train, bleeding out over a closed door. He was declared dead at a nearby hospital.

Months earlier, on the same Western Railway line, 33-year-old Alok Kumar Singh — a college professor — was stabbed as his train approached Malad station, in a dispute that began with him trying to get off. He died from his injuries. Just days after Mayank's death, near Mahim station, another man was chased down and stabbed after nothing more than an accidental brush in a crowd. He survived. He was lucky. But Luck shouldn't be the deciding factor in whether you go home tonight.

These weren't planned crimes. They were arguments that someone decided to end with a blade instead of a sentence.

## The Problem: A Country Running on Borrowed Calm

Somewhere along the way, the public space stopped feeling shared and started feeling like a minefield. Every train compartment, every signal crossing, every office cubicle now carries the same quiet, unspoken question: *will this person snap if I say something?*

This isn't paranoia. It's pattern recognition, built incident by incident, headline by headline. And it isn't only happening on Mumbai locals. It's happening at home, where a raised voice over a missed deadline now carries real risk. It's happening in schools and colleges, where disagreements that once ended in a complaint to a teacher now sometimes end in a hospital visit. It's happening in workplaces, where a corrected mistake is taken not as feedback, but as a personal attack on someone's dignity.

### Why a Closed Door Becomes a Death Sentence

None of this happens in isolation. Anger is, in clinical terms, a completely normal human emotion — the APA describes it as a state that varies from mild irritation to full rage, accompanied by a real physiological storm: heart rate spikes, blood pressure climbs, stress hormones flood the system. The problem isn't that anger exists. The problem is what happens when there's nothing left to absorb it.

Psychology Today's research into rising global anger points to seven quiet, cumulative pressures stacking on top of each other in nearly everyone's life right now: we are **tired** — about **one in three adults don't get enough sleep**, and poor sleep directly predicts more intense anger; we are **overwhelmed**, buried under more demands on our attention than ever before; we are **afraid**, because fear and anger share the same neural pathway, with fear quite literally promoting anger in the brain; we are living through **record heat**, and researchers have found rising temperatures directly increase aggressive behavior; we are stuck in a **zero-sum world** where even a parking spot feels like combat; we are **financially stretched**, and money worries are directly linked to psychological distress; and many of us are simply living **in pain** — physical, chronic, draining pain that wears patience down to nothing.

Now stack a stranger's bad day on top of your own. The man on the train wasn't just angry about a door. He was angry about everything that door represented — exhaustion, disrespect, a long week, a long year — and a 22-year-old's ordinary request became the one straw that broke something already cracked.

### "You Disrespected Me" — The Mentality Behind the Knife

Here's the part that's hardest to accept: increasingly, people aren't reacting to what was actually said. They're reacting to what they've decided it meant. A request to shut a door becomes an order. A request to stop pushing becomes an insult. A simple correction becomes proof that someone thinks you're foolish or beneath them — even when nothing in the sentence implied that.

When ego becomes the lens through which every interaction is filtered, even the most neutral request — "please move," "keep this closed," "stop pushing" — gets translated into a threat that must be answered, not a sentence that can simply be agreed with or politely declined.</p>
</div>

And the tragedy is that almost none of the victims in these stories did anything wrong. Mayank didn't provoke anyone. He made a reasonable request that thousands of commuters make every day during Mumbai's monsoon. Alok Singh simply tried to get off a train at his own stop. These were not victims who escalated a conflict. They were ordinary people who got unlucky enough to stand near someone whose anger had nowhere else to go.

This is what makes these stories so unbearable to sit with. The difficult truth is this: positive, ordinary, decent behavior doesn't trend. It isn't shocking, so it isn't shared. A man giving up his seat for an elderly passenger, a stranger helping someone find their platform, a thousand peaceful train rides that happened the same night Mayank died — none of that makes the news. Only the knife does. And so the algorithm of our attention slowly convinces us that the knife is more common than it actually is, even as the real underlying numbers — thousands of dispute-driven murders every year — confirm that the fear isn't entirely manufactured either.

### How We Learned to Disappear in Public

The response to all this hasn't been outrage. It's been withdrawal. People have quietly started rewriting the rules of how to exist in public, and the new rules all point the same direction: become smaller, become quieter, become invisible.

- "Don't honk."
- "Don't complain."
- "Don't correct someone."
- "Just get home."

And here is the part that should genuinely disturb us: this isn't irrational. In the heat of a single moment, it is often the safest possible choice. Legal experts studying India's bystander problem have found that **88% of people believe a Good Samaritan will face harassment from police or hospitals** simply for stepping in to help — endless questioning, court summons, hours lost to a system that punishes the very people trying to do the right thing. Even after the Supreme Court introduced a Good Samaritan Law in 2016 to protect helpers from this harassment, **77% of people remain too afraid of the police to act on it.**

Each of those numbers represents someone who needed a stranger to care enough to stop — and the data shows fear is the single biggest reason they don't.

So the silence isn't apathy. It's armor. Somewhere between Nirbhaya in 2012, a man run over repeatedly on a Delhi road while pedestrians watched in 2017, and a string of train stabbings in 2026, an entire population quietly concluded that getting involved is a gamble no longer worth taking. Your life, the thinking goes, is worth more than someone else's ego, more than a moral lesson. So you say sorry — even when it wasn't your fault — and you move on, because you don't actually know what's sitting inside the stranger in front of you, and you'd rather not find out the hard way.

## The Solution: Refusing to Let Fear Write the Whole Story

Here is where this gets complicated, and honestly, uncomfortable: silence sometimes really is the wiser choice in the moment. Nobody reading this should feel obligated to confront a stranger holding a knife, or to honk at a driver who might be one bad night away from doing something irreversible. De-escalation is not cowardice. Choosing to breathe instead of react can genuinely save your life — and the APA's own anger research backs this completely. Their recommended first response to rising anger in someone else, or in yourself, is not confrontation. It's distance: take a breath, observe, then decide how to proceed, rather than letting the first instinct win.

But there's a difference between choosing silence in a single dangerous moment and adopting silence as a permanent worldview. The first is self-preservation. The second is surrender. And if an entire society quietly decides that no one should ever object to anything or hold a stranger accountable for fear of what they might do — then we haven't just lost a few uncomfortable conversations. We've lost the invisible thread of trust that lets millions of strangers share a train, a road, and a city every day without needing armed guards between them.

### What the Anger Researchers Actually Recommend

The good news, if there is any in a piece like this, is that anger itself is enormously treatable — both individually and socially. The APA reports that with proper support, a highly angry person can typically shift into a calmer range of emotion within just **8 to 10 weeks** of structured counseling. Across hundreds of studies, roughly **75% of people who go through anger management therapy show real improvement**. This isn't a permanent trait we're powerless against. It's a pattern that responds to attention.

None of this erases what happened to Mayank Lohar or Alok Kumar Singh. No framework, no statistic, no breathing technique brings back a 22-year-old who only wanted to stay dry on his way home. But understanding why this is happening — the exhaustion, the heat, the financial pressure, the ego wound that turns a sentence into a threat, the very real fear that keeps good people silent — is the first step toward refusing to let it become the new normal.

Healthy anger is in the moment. It protects your boundaries, and then it's gone. The problem isn't that we feel anger — it's that too many of us have nowhere left to put it down.

## The Bottom Line

Why does it feel like the world has quietly shifted from something to live in to something to survive through? When did we start looking at every other person in a queue, on a train, in traffic, and thinking, *I hope this one doesn't snap?* When did "please" become a gamble?

Maybe the honest answer isn't blind optimism, and pretending the danger isn't real would be its own kind of dishonesty. But the answer also isn't surrendering every public space to the worst version of human behavior. It's refusing to let the loudest, most violent stories define what humanity actually is — while still being smart enough to protect yourself in the moment that calls for it. Because the day fear becomes our only lens for looking at each other, the man with the knife has already taken something far larger than one life. He's taken our willingness to trust the stranger standing next to us. And that, multiplied across millions of train rides and ordinary days, is a loss this country cannot quietly absorb forever.
