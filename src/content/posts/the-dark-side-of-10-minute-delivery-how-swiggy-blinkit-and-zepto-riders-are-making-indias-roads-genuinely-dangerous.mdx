You ordered onions at 9 PM. A teenager from Bihar, on his first day in the city, is now racing against a 10-minute clock on a potholed road at rush hour — because if he's late, his rating drops, his earnings fall, and his shift may not be renewed. And if he clips your car on the way, neither of you has a clear answer for what happens next.

## It happened right in front of you. And it'll happen again tomorrow.

Picture this. You're in the left lane, waiting at a signal, doing exactly what you're supposed to. The light turns green. You start moving. And before you've covered ten metres, a yellow-jacketed rider cuts across the divider from the wrong side, misses your front bumper by half an arm's length, doesn't look back, and disappears into the gap between two autos. Your heart is still hammering three signals later. The delivery app on his phone is already showing the next order.

This is not a freak incident. It is now a daily feature of commuting in every major Indian city — Bengaluru, Delhi, Mumbai, Hyderabad, Pune, Kolkata. The colours change — orange for Swiggy, yellow for Blinkit, green for Zepto, red and white for Zomato — but the behaviour is identical: wrong side, no helmet, red light ignored, weaving at speed, always, always in a hurry. And the rest of us are left to absorb the risk of a business model that is not ours, for a convenience we did not ask for.

## This is not about bad riders. It's about a broken system.

Let's get one thing clear from the start: the delivery rider who cuts you off at that signal is not a villain. He is almost certainly earning less than ₹10,000 a month. More than 68% of gig delivery workers in India report that their monthly expenses exceed their earnings. He is working 12 to 15-hour shifts with no paid leave, no pension, no formal employment protection, and no guaranteed income. He is not riding recklessly because he doesn't care about your safety — he is riding recklessly because the system he works inside has been engineered to make speed the only variable that controls his income.

The quick commerce industry in India is now one of the fastest-growing economic sectors on Earth. Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart, Zepto, BigBasket Now, Flipkart Minutes — they are all racing to dominate a market expected to cross $50 billion by 2030. As of early 2025, Blinkit and Swiggy Instamart together operate more than 3,000 dark stores across India, with both companies having publicly committed to doubling those numbers. More dark stores mean more orders, which means more riders on every road, in every city, at every hour of the day and night.

## The 10-minute deadline: a productivity target that became a death sentence

When Zomato first floated the idea of 10-minute deliveries under "Zomato Instant" in 2022, it was met with widespread derision and concern. Riders, safety experts and consumer advocates all pointed out that 10 minutes was physically impossible on congested Indian roads without breaking multiple traffic laws. The company quietly shelved the branding. Then in October 2024, Swiggy launched its "Bolt" service with explicit 10-minute food delivery promises in Bengaluru — and the floodgates opened. Within weeks, Blinkit and Zepto announced equivalent services. Amazon India entered the market in December 2024 with a 15-minute delivery service called "Tez" — Hindi for fast.

For the riders, none of this represented an upgrade. It represented a tightening of a vise that was already crushing them. Ratings are everything in the gig economy: a higher rating means better shift access, earlier order assignment, and eligibility for bonus incentive structures. A lower rating means fewer orders, lower earnings, and the risk of being cut from the platform entirely. The rating system penalises lateness. The incentive structure rewards speed. The traffic outside does not move any faster because an app has decided your groceries should arrive in ten minutes. So the rider does the only thing the system has left room for: he drives on the wrong side, he jumps the signal, he weaves through the gap, and he prays nothing goes wrong.

## The hidden costs that nobody prices into the delivery fee

When you pay ₹0 delivery fee for a bottle of ketchup that arrives in 12 minutes, you are not seeing the full invoice. The true cost of that delivery is distributed across people who never consented to pay it. The rider may absorb it physically — in the form of a crash, an injury, or the slow deterioration of working 14-hour days at sustained stress. The rider's family may absorb it financially — motor accident compensation cases in India's claims tribunals take 13 to 20 months to resolve, leaving injured workers and their dependants without income in the meantime. The other motorists on the road — you, driving home after work — absorb it in the form of near-misses, elevated stress, and the genuine fear of being hit.

India recorded 4,64,029 road accidents in 2023, resulting in 1,73,826 deaths and 4.47 lakh injuries, according to NCRB data. Nearly 46% of those victims were two-wheeler riders. Over-speeding caused 58.6% of deaths. Dangerous driving and overtaking accounted for another 23.6%. Two-wheeler deaths specifically — the category that includes virtually all delivery riders — have nearly doubled over the last decade, rising from 30% of all road deaths in 2014 to 45% by 2023. Nine two-wheeler riders die on Indian roads every single hour. These numbers are a national emergency in plain sight, and the rise of a delivery ecosystem that runs on speed is accelerating every one of these trends.

## What the platforms say — and what they actually do

Zomato, when contacted by IndiaSpend, stated: "Our processes are designed to prioritise safety over speed. Riders complete a mandatory road safety module, are told not to overspeed or break traffic rules, and are not penalised for delays." It also mentioned health and accident insurance, ambulance assistance, and rest points. These are genuine provisions. They also exist in a system where the incentive architecture — bonuses for completing high volumes, ratings that fall when deliveries are late — runs in the exact opposite direction. If a rider is not penalised for being late, why are 70%+ of surveyed gig workers admitting to breaking traffic rules to meet time targets? The gap between official policy and lived experience on the road is not subtle. It is a chasm, and it is measured in the 17,218 traffic cases Bengaluru police booked against delivery riders in a single week.

Swiggy offers health insurance and Blinkit offers bonus pay after 26 days of continuous duty — both meaningful benefits. But one rider described it starkly: "I was once hit by a car when I was on my way to a customer. My bike took time to restart, so I was 20 minutes late. I got a bad rating." The system rewarded neither his survival nor his continued presence. It penalised his delay. That incentive structure, not the absence of a helmet or a disregard for rules, is the root cause of what you experience every morning on your commute.

## This is fixable. But it requires pressure from every direction simultaneously.

The good news — if there is any in this picture — is that the problem is not a mystery. Its causes are visible, its consequences are documented, and the solutions are not technologically complex. What is missing is accountability: from the platforms, from regulators, and increasingly, from consumers who have the power to demand change through the choices they make.

In January 2026, the Indian government did signal that it was watching. Advisories were sent to quick commerce platforms asking them to curb the 10-minute delivery promise. The language was advisory, not mandatory — and as Al Jazeera documented, riders continued to be held to de facto 10-minute windows through incentive structures, even as the marketing language was softened. Real regulatory intervention — with teeth — has not yet arrived. The Gig Workers' Welfare Code, repeatedly discussed in policy circles, remains unlegislated.

## What you can do — right now, as a consumer

You have more power in this system than you realise. When you tip your delivery rider, you are telling the platform that the human being on the motorcycle matters. When you choose standard delivery over express, you reduce the impossible pressure on one rider tonight. When you leave a review that explicitly mentions the rider's safety behaviour — positively, when he arrives calmly rather than frantically — you shift the rating dynamic in a tiny but meaningful way. And when you call your local corporator, file a complaint on the Traffic Police portal, or sign a petition demanding platform accountability regulation, you are doing something the platforms have enormous incentive to prevent: holding the business model to the same standards as the rest of the road.

The rage you feel when a Blinkit rider cuts across your lane at 60 kmph is legitimate. Direct some of it at the algorithm that created his deadline, not just the teenager trying to survive it.

## Don't just be frustrated. Be heard.

Every near-miss you experience is data. Every reckless rider you see is a symptom of a system that treats a teenager's life as a logistics variable. If this resonates with you — as a commuter, as a citizen, as someone who has nearly been hit — here is what you can do today:
