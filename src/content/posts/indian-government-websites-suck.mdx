## The Tatkal Ticket You Couldn't Book, Explained in Full

10 a.m when the Tatkal reservation window opens.

You sign in. Enter all the details. But the loading wheel keeps spinning for 45 seconds after you click "Book Now." Boom

The session expires.

However, those seats were taken by someone. How did they accomplish it, then?

This is not a rare edge case. This is the daily reality for millions of Indians trying to interact with their own government online. The session that expired, the captcha that failed, the payment that processed but the booking that didn't — these are not bugs that slipped through testing. They are the predictable output of a system that was never built to serve the person actually using it.

And before this becomes another "Indian government is incompetent" rant: the problem is not incompetence. It is structure. It is incentive. It is a procurement system so spectacularly broken that it almost guarantees the worst possible outcome at every step.

## The L1 Tender System: Why India Hires the Worst Developer on Purpose

When a government department needs a website, it does not hire the best team available. It is legally required to hire the cheapest one. This is the L1 procurement system — "L1" stands for "Lowest Bidder 1" — and it is the single most important explanation for why Indian government websites look and behave the way they do.

A tender is floated. Specifications are written by bureaucrats who often have limited technical understanding of what they need. Multiple vendors bid. The contract goes to whoever quotes the lowest number. This sounds fiscally responsible until you think through what actually happens: the only vendors who can afford to quote the lowest price are either those cutting extreme corners on security, testing, infrastructure, and talent — or those planning to make money on "change request" invoices submitted after the initial work is complete. Either way, what gets built is the minimum viable product that technically meets the letter of the specification, not the spirit of serving citizens.

<div class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6 sm:p-7">
  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">
    <span aria-hidden="true">🔍</span> Conway's Law — Why Chaotic Offices Build Chaotic Websites
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">There's a famous principle in software engineering called Conway's Law: organisations design systems that mirror their own internal communication structures. If a physical government office requires you to visit five different windows, collect three separate stamps, and navigate competing departments that don't speak to each other — the website built by that organisation will replicate that exact chaos, digitally. The dysfunctional process doesn't become a clean app. It becomes a dysfunctional app. Every unnecessary page, every confusing form, every "session expired" is Conway's Law in action.</p>
</div>

The problem compounds because there is typically no dedicated team maintaining the site after it's built. A vendor delivers, the contract ends, and the website sits unchanged until either something breaks spectacularly or someone floats a new tender. There is rarely a UX designer, a product manager, or a security auditor whose full-time job is making that particular website better. There is just a live URL that nobody owns.

  <p class="text-lg font-medium italic leading-relaxed text-ink">"IRCTC is the only place where you can be logged out while logging in. You fill out a captcha that looks like it's written in Sanskrit, get OTP'd twice, pick your train, click 'Book Now' and boom! Session expired."</p>
  <footer class="mt-4 text-sm font-bold text-mute">— Sudhanshu Kanth, designer, on Medium</footer>

This isn't hyperbole. It's a documented design failure affecting hundreds of millions of attempted transactions annually on what should be one of India's most critical public services.

## You Are Not the Target User. Your Neta Is.

Open any state government website in search of job listings. Before you reach the actual information, you will scroll past a large banner photo of the Chief Minister, a gallery of recent "achievements," a news section curated for press coverage, and possibly a welcome message in video form. The jobs section, if it exists, comes several scrolls later. The design is not organised around what the user needs. It is organised around what the website's political patron wants to communicate.

This is not a coincidence. Government websites serve two masters: the citizens who need information, and the officials who control the budgets. The officials are the ones who approve the design briefs, sign off on the wireframes, and make the go-live call. If they would like their photograph at the top of every page, it will be there. If they feel that the "Schemes Launched by CM Sahib" section should be given more prominence than the application portal, that is what the developer delivers. The result is a website that is functionally better at broadcasting political communication than at helping anyone actually do anything.

## Your Data Is Not Safe. Here's the Evidence.

When a government website looks old, you might wonder if it's secure. The answer, based on the track record, is that it frequently is not — and the scale of the failures is staggering.

<div class="not-prose my-8 grid grid-cols-1 gap-4 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div class="rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-error bg-error/5 p-6">
    <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-error-deep">
      <span aria-hidden="true">🔓</span> ICMR Data Breach — 81 Crore Indians (2023)
    </p>
    <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Personal data of 81.5 crore Indians — including Aadhaar numbers, passport details, phone numbers, and COVID-19 test records — was found for sale on the dark web for $80,000. The breach is one of the largest in any country's recorded history. The ICMR website had reportedly sustained over 6,000 attempted hacks in the year prior, yet remained unprotected. The source of the leak has still not been definitively identified.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-error bg-error/5 p-6">
    <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-error-deep">
      <span aria-hidden="true">🔓</span> IRCTC Passenger Data Leak — 30 Million Records
    </p>
    <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Over 30 million railway passenger records were compromised in a single breach — names, contact information, and travel history for crores of ordinary Indians. The breach occurred due to database vulnerabilities and a lack of proper access controls. These were not sophisticated state-level cyberattacks. They were basic security hygiene failures.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-error bg-error/5 p-6">
    <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-error-deep">
      <span aria-hidden="true">🔓</span> CoWIN Data Leak — COVID Vaccination Records (2023)
    </p>
    <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">In 2023, citizens' COVID-19 vaccination data from the CoWIN portal was found circulating on Telegram — accessible to anyone who knew where to look. The platform had been the central infrastructure for tracking one of history's largest vaccination drives. The breach included names, Aadhaar numbers, dates of vaccination, and health centre details.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-error bg-error/5 p-6">
    <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-error-deep">
      <span aria-hidden="true">🔓</span> 210+ State and Central Sites Exposing Aadhaar (Ongoing)
    </p>
    <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">The government itself revealed in Parliament that over 210 central and state government websites were displaying Aadhaar numbers and PAN card details of beneficiaries publicly — in plain text, accessible by anyone who found the page. Scholarship databases in Uttar Pradesh, pension dashboards in Kerala, and Swachh Bharat scheme portals were among those found publicly displaying sensitive citizen data.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-error bg-error/5 p-6 sm:p-7">
  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-error-deep">
    <span aria-hidden="true">⚠️</span> India's Cybersecurity Position — 2025
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">In the first quarter of 2025, India emerged as one of the top five nations targeted by ransomware, with a 126% year-over-year surge in attacks. India was the second most targeted nation for cybercrime in all of 2024, trailing only the US. The Union government's own data shows cybersecurity incidents in India rose from 10.29 lakh in 2022 to 22.68 lakh in 2024 — a 120% increase in two years. The same government that reports these numbers runs the infrastructure where the breaches keep happening.</p>
</div>

## The UK Did Something About This. India Can Too.

The UK Government Digital Service (GDS), founded in 2011, was built around a radical idea: government websites should work as well as the best private-sector websites, and should be designed around what users need rather than what departments want to publish. The result — GOV.UK — is now consistently cited as one of the best government digital experiences in the world. Clean typography, no clutter, no politician portraits, no irrelevant galleries. Just information, searchable and usable, designed by full-time professional designers, engineers, and user researchers who have no other job.

## What Would Actually Help — Five Specific Steps

The problem isn't unsolvable. India already has the talent — the same engineers who built UPI, DigiLocker, and COWIN (when it worked) are proof of that. The question is whether the political will exists to change the incentive structures that produce bad outcomes by design. Here's what would actually make a difference.

<div class="not-prose my-8 space-y-5">
  <div class="flex gap-4 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-cat-politics text-sm font-extrabold text-white dark:bg-cat-politics-dark">1</span>
    <div>
      <p class="text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">Kill the L1 Tender System for Technology Projects</p>
      <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Replace lowest-bidder procurement for digital projects with a Quality and Cost-Based Selection (QCBS) system. The technical quality of a bid — architecture, security design, maintenance plan, developer credentials — should carry significantly more weight than price. A government website handling 100 million users a year is not a commodity procurement. Treating it as one is the origin of almost every problem described in this article.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="flex gap-4 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-cat-politics text-sm font-extrabold text-white dark:bg-cat-politics-dark">2</span>
    <div>
      <p class="text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">Create a Central Government Digital Service (Indian GDS)</p>
      <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">India needs an equivalent of the UK's GDS: a permanent team of full-time designers, engineers, product managers, and security specialists whose exclusive mandate is to own government digital infrastructure. Not a consulting firm brought in on a three-month contract. A permanent body with budget, authority, and accountability. The team that built COWIN exists — it needs a permanent home and mandate.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="flex gap-4 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-cat-politics text-sm font-extrabold text-white dark:bg-cat-politics-dark">3</span>
    <div>
      <p class="text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">Mandate Load Testing Before Every Predictable Traffic Event</p>
      <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">The CBSE results page crashes every year on a date that is known months in advance. This is not an unpredictable disaster — it is a failure to load-test for known peak traffic. Every government site that experiences predictable annual traffic spikes (exam results, tax deadlines, train booking windows, vaccination registrations) should be required to demonstrate load capacity before going live for that cycle. This is a basic engineering practice that is simply not being enforced.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="flex gap-4 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-cat-politics text-sm font-extrabold text-white dark:bg-cat-politics-dark">4</span>
    <div>
      <p class="text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">Mandatory Security Audits and Public Disclosure</p>
      <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Every government website handling citizen PII (Aadhaar, PAN, health data, financial data) should be independently audited for security annually, with findings published publicly — similar to how listed companies publish audit reports. The current model of discovering breaches after millions of records are already for sale on dark web forums is not a security posture. It is an absence of one. Public accountability would create real incentives for departments to prioritise security before breaches, not after.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="flex gap-4 rounded-2xl border border-hairline bg-surface-card p-6">
    <span class="flex h-8 w-8 shrink-0 items-center justify-center rounded-full bg-cat-politics text-sm font-extrabold text-white dark:bg-cat-politics-dark">5</span>
    <div>
      <p class="text-sm font-extrabold text-ink">Ban Politician Photographs and Political Content from Functional Portals</p>
      <p class="mt-2 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">This sounds simple but would be culturally significant. A government website that helps citizens access a scheme, file a document, or book a ticket is not a campaign microsite. A clear mandate that functional portals must open directly to the citizen's task — not to a welcome message or CM photograph — would force a design reorientation from "what the department wants to say" to "what the user needs to do." The UK achieved this with a simple design standard. India can too.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-bull bg-bull/5 p-6 sm:p-7">
  <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-bull dark:text-bull-dark">
    <span aria-hidden="true">✅</span> The Honest Bottom Line
  </p>
  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">The real scandal isn't that the sites look old. Old is fine. The scandal is that they don't work — and they don't work for the same reason they've never worked: they were built cheaply, by vendors with no ongoing accountability, for departments whose incentives were never aligned with the person actually using the service. Changing that requires structural reform, not a fresh coat of CSS paint. India has the talent. It needs the system to stop wasting it.</p>
</div>
