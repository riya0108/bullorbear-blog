Picture a man who built schools from mud and solar panels in one of the world's harshest climates, who created artificial glaciers with bamboo pipes so farmers could survive drought, who inspired a generation through a Bollywood character — and who is now lying in a hospital bed in Delhi, writing notes by hand because a government refuses to sit across a table from him.

He didn't want to be here. That is perhaps the most important thing to understand about Sonam Wangchuk. He is, by training and by calling, a builder. An educator. An innovator. He built **SECMOL** — the Students' Educational and Cultural Movement of Ladakh — in 1988, when he was 22, transforming a generation of children who had been labelled failures by a system that taught them in languages they didn't speak. He invented the **Ice Stupa** in 2013: conical artificial glaciers built in winter, slowly melting into spring, delivering water to farmers in Ladakh's arid terrain when they need it most. In 2018, he won the **Ramon Magsaysay Award** — Asia's equivalent of the Nobel Prize. His story became Phunsukh Wangdu in *3 Idiots*. He should be at his institute right now, with his students. Instead, he has been on hunger strike for over 22 days.

## What He Is Actually Asking For

Before forming an opinion, every citizen deserves to know what the demands actually are. Not the characterisation of those demands — the demands themselves.

### For Ladakh (ongoing since 2019):

1. Full statehood for Ladakh — so it has an elected legislature and is no longer governed entirely from Delhi.
2. Inclusion under the Sixth Schedule of the Constitution — granting tribal communities constitutional safeguards over land, resources, and local governance.
3. A separate Public Service Commission for Ladakh — so local people can compete fairly for government jobs without fighting against a national pool.
4. Dedicated parliamentary seats to ensure Ladakhi voices are heard in Parliament.

### For education (June–July 2026):

5. Accountability for the NEET UG examination paper leak — which allegedly compromised a national-level entrance exam for medical education, affecting hundreds of thousands of students.
6. Resignation of Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan over systemic exam irregularities.

Since 2019, it has cited increased ST reservations (from 45% to 84%), recognition of Bhoti and Purgi as official languages, and recruitment of 1,800 posts as evidence of progress. But Wangchuk and Ladakh's civil society maintain that these are concessions on peripheral issues while the core constitutional demands — statehood and Sixth Schedule — remain unaddressed. Talks have been started and then abruptly halted by the Centre on multiple occasions.</p>

## What the Law Says — And Why It's Complicated

Hunger strikes as a form of protest have deep roots in India's democratic and freedom tradition — from Gandhi to Potti Sriramulu, whose 58-day fast in 1952 directly led to the creation of Andhra Pradesh. The legal framework around them has changed significantly.

<div class="not-prose my-8 grid grid-cols-1 gap-5 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div class="rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-error bg-error/5 p-6">
    <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-error-deep">
      <span aria-hidden="true">🔴</span> Then — Irom Sharmila's Era
    </p>
    <p class="mt-4 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">Under Section 309 of the old IPC, attempting suicide was a criminal offence. Sharmila was arrested, kept in judicial custody, and force-fed via a nasogastric tube for <strong class="text-ink">16 continuous years</strong>. Released annually, she would pull out her feeding tube and resume fasting immediately — only to be re-arrested days later.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-bull bg-bull/5 p-6">
    <p class="flex items-center gap-2 text-sm font-extrabold text-bull dark:text-bull-dark">
      <span aria-hidden="true">✅</span> Now — 2026 Framework
    </p>
    <p class="mt-4 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita replaced the IPC. <strong class="text-ink">Attempted suicide is no longer a criminal offence.</strong> Courts have also become more protective of bodily autonomy. But the state still has obligations — the Delhi High Court ordered daily health monitoring and medical assistance "whenever necessary."</p>
  </div>
</div>

In Wangchuk's case, the Delhi High Court directed authorities to monitor his health daily. The police shifted him to Safdarjung Hospital on Day 21, citing this order. His family disputes the interpretation — saying he was forcibly removed against his will and their knowledge. His wife has asked doctors not to administer IV treatment without family consent. The Supreme Court has, in multiple rulings, consistently *encouraged governments to engage through dialogue* rather than confrontation with hunger strikers.

<blockquote class="not-prose my-8 rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-cat-politics bg-surface-card p-6 dark:border-cat-politics-dark sm:p-7">
  <p class="text-lg font-medium italic leading-relaxed text-ink">The best-known case involving force-feeding is that of Irom Sharmila. Legal experts stress her case involved state custody. A public protest like Wangchuk's operates under a different framework entirely.</p>
  <footer class="mt-4 text-sm font-bold text-mute">— The News Minute, July 2026 legal analysis</footer>
</blockquote>

## This Is Not Really About Politics Anymore

Here is where this blog has to step back from the timeline and ask the harder question. Whether you agree with Sonam Wangchuk's specific demands or not — whether you think the NEET controversy has been blown out of proportion, or whether you believe Ladakh's constitutional status is an issue for another day — none of that is the central question anymore.

The central question is this: **[Why did one man have to go this far just to be heard?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xC9NkYMAQQ8)**

He tried dialogue — it stalled. He tried a 21-day fast in sub-zero temperatures — it was ignored. He tried marching 1,000 kilometres — he was detained at the border. He tried another hunger strike — he was put under the National Security Act. He tried again — and on Day 21, he was pulled away from a protest site at dawn and taken to a hospital he says he didn't consent to entering. Through all of this, no senior government representative has sat down with him for a substantive, on-the-record conversation about his core demands.

  <p class="mt-3 text-sm leading-relaxed text-body">The Centre maintains that it has engaged continuously with Ladakhi representatives through formal committees, delivered measurable results (reservation increases, language recognition, jobs), and that Wangchuk's hunger strikes are political theatre escalated by opposition parties. The government says dialogue channels remain open. It has not publicly addressed why the core constitutional demands have produced no formal response in six years.</p>

Disagreeing with Wangchuk's methods or his politics is a completely legitimate position. But disagreeing without knowing his story is something else. Supporting him without understanding what he's asking for is also something else. *Both require knowledge first.*

Positive acts of accountability don't trend. A meeting held, a demand addressed, a promise kept — none of that goes viral. A man being dragged from a protest site on Day 21 of a hunger strike does. That asymmetry isn't new. But it means the burden of being informed falls entirely on us, as citizens, to seek out the full picture rather than the shock frame.
