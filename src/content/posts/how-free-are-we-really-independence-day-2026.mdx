Just before midnight on August 14th, 1947, Jawaharlal Nehru stood up in front of India's newly formed Constituent Assembly and said the sentence every Indian schoolchild eventually memorises, whether or not anyone ever makes them:

"At the stroke of the midnight hour, while the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom."
</PostCallout>

What gets quoted far less often is the line that came right after it — the part where he said the pledge India had made was only partly redeemed, and that the real work was only just beginning. Eighty years on, on India's 80th Independence Day, it's that second half of the sentence worth sitting with. Not the part about waking up free. The part about the work still owed.

## Freedom Then, Freedom Now

In 1947, freedom had a short, brutal checklist. No more foreign rule, obviously — but underneath that, it meant surviving a Partition that displaced more people, more suddenly, than almost any migration in recorded history, and killed a number historians still argue over decades later. It meant writing a Constitution, printing a currency, raising an army, and standing up a functioning government almost from nothing, for a country of over 340 million people, most of whom had never once cast a vote in their lives. Freedom, in 1947, was survival plus sovereignty. Everything else could wait its turn.

Eighty years later, with a population of 1.45 billion and an economy among the largest on earth, that version of freedom has, for the most part, been won. Nobody seriously debates anymore whether India is a sovereign nation. What's left to argue about is quieter, and in some ways harder to solve than colonial withdrawal ever was — freedom from unemployment, freedom from misinformation, freedom from fear, the freedom to actually question the institutions this country spent eighty years building, and underneath all of it, the freedom to simply build a better life. None of that fits on a flag. All of it is still, stubbornly, unfinished.

And "unfinished" doesn't mean the same thing to everyone. Ask a twenty-five-year-old at a tech park in Bengaluru whether India feels free, and the honest answer is probably yes, close to entirely — global travel, private healthcare, an English-medium education, a passport that opens doors without a second glance. That India is real, and it deserves to be said plainly, without irony: 24.8 crore people climbed out of multidimensional poverty between 2013 and 2022, according to NITI Aayog's own figures, one of the fastest declines in poverty any country has recorded in that short a stretch of time.

## Freedom Depends On Who's Asking

But ask the same question of someone still among the roughly one in twenty Indians living in extreme poverty, and freedom starts competing with more basic things — the freedom to eat properly, to stay in school past a certain age, to see a doctor without borrowing money to do it. Ask a woman, and the calculus changes again. Ask someone among India's roughly 27 million persons with disabilities, and freedom often means fighting infrastructure before fighting anything else — a 2025 industry study found that persons with disabilities make up less than one percent of the workforce across corporate India, not because the talent isn't there, but because the ramps, the hiring processes, and the assumptions usually aren't.

So "how free are we" was never really one question. It's five or six, asked at once, with five or six honest answers that are all technically true at the same time.

## When India Asked Its Institutions to Answer

This June and July, a lot of those different Indias ended up asking the same question out loud, together, on the same streets. It started small — a leaked medical entrance exam, the kind nearly two million students had spent a year preparing for, thrown into doubt overnight. What began as outrage over one exam grew, within weeks, into something much larger: a youth movement, half-satirical in its name and entirely serious in its intent, camped near Parliament and demanding accountability for the leak.

It wasn't a comfortable seven weeks. But there was also, at the end of it, this: an institution, faced with a country that simply refused to stop asking, actually answered.

<PostCallout variant="quote">
Patriotism was never supposed to mean pretending everything is fine. It means wanting things to be better, and saying so, loudly, in public, without apologising for it. A country isn't strong because nobody ever questions it. A country is strong because it can be questioned — and because its institutions, eventually, have to answer back.
</PostCallout>

## What Bhagat Singh Would Post Today

There's an old, useful ghost worth invoking here. Bhagat Singh was twenty-three — younger than most people finishing a postgraduate degree today — when he was hanged in Lahore Central Jail in March 1931. In his last months, waiting in a cell for his own execution, he didn't spend his remaining time writing slogans. He wrote an essay called *"Why I Am an Atheist,"* calmly taking apart the religious certainties of his own family, questioning why anyone — himself included — should submit to any authority, divine or colonial, without first demanding a reason.

So here's a strange, useful question for 2026: if Bhagat Singh had Instagram today, what would he actually post? Would he simply post a flag, a filter, a caption wishing everyone a Happy Independence Day, and move on with his day? Or would he do exactly what he spent his short, deliberate life doing — question the world in front of him? Question why a country producing this many engineers still has this much youth unemployment. Question why exam papers keep leaking. Question why a woman still needs someone's permission to leave her own house. Question, and keep questioning, until somebody with power was finally forced to answer.

## The Six Things Still Broken

Ask any young Indian, off the record, what actually feels broken, and the list rarely changes much from person to person. It's usually some version of the same six things: an education system where a single leaked exam can spark a nationwide movement; an inequality that keeps widening in absolute terms even as the national averages genuinely improve; a corruption that shows up, survey after survey, as the single biggest reason honest effort doesn't translate into a fair outcome; a justice system slow enough that "tareek pe tareek" — the next date, and the next, and the next — became a courtroom cliché for a reason; a press freedom that has measurably worsened, with India dropping six places in a single year to rank 157th out of 180 countries in the 2026 World Press Freedom Index; and underneath all of it, a youth unemployment rate that touches nearly 18 percent in urban India, and over one in four for young women specifically — a whole generation being asked to dream big on a runway that keeps getting shorter.

None of this cancels out the other India — the one that lifted a quarter of a billion people out of poverty in under a decade, that built its own vaccine during a pandemic, that landed near the moon's south pole for less than the cost of a Hollywood film. Both Indias are real at the same time. Neither one, alone, is the whole truth.

## The Only Kind of Patriotism That Keeps the Promise

Maybe the most patriotic thing anyone can do this Independence Day isn't posting "Jai Hind."

Maybe it's caring enough to ask why. Why the exam leaked. Why she isn't allowed to walk home alone after dark. Why the ramp outside the office was never built. Why the case has been "pending" for eleven years. Asking, and then staying around long enough to actually hear the answer — and pushing back, again, when the first answer isn't good enough.

That kind of patriotism doesn't photograph as well as a flag filter. It doesn't fit neatly in a caption. But eighty years after a midnight promise that was only ever partly redeemed, it might be the only kind that actually keeps the promise moving.

Happy 80th Independence Day, India. Here's to a freedom that keeps growing into everyone it was always meant to include. Jai Hind.
